# Verifying an AI-drafted laboratory-interpretation rule base before clinical audit: executable invariants, frozen source-concordance protocols and a hash-frozen benchmark applied to the open Hemoglyph engine

**Highlights**
- Four frozen, reproducible methods verify an AI-drafted lab rule base pre-review
- Executable invariants found a ranking defect that 353 adversarial vignettes missed
- Frozen provenance audits: 92% of rules unverifiable by locator; 1.1% drive output
- Unit-invariant in 40/40 panels vs 8/40 for an LLM; cuts LLM unsupported claims 40%
- Held-out mutation test and one-command clean-clone reproduction; no clinical claim

## Abstract

**Background and Objective:** An LLM-drafted clinical rule base is a set of unread hypotheses about what guidelines say; vignette validation misses mistranscription and ranking defects, and reviewing every rule is impractical. We asked what can be established *before* such review, for Hemoglyph, an offline engine that ranks a cited laboratory-panel differential with 33,183 rules (99.2% AI-drafted).

**Methods:** Four pre-frozen methods: invariant tests (determinism, unit invariance, monotonicity, absent analyte) on random panels, on real panels shared with an LLM and as a verifier behind it; a source-concordance audit of 200 sampled rules, then of 100 rules an activity log over 3,814 real panels showed drive output; a hash-frozen one-shot benchmark on 2,314 ICU stays, 9,000 NHANES adults, 14,420 sepsis patients and external cohorts against supervised, prevalence and blinded LLM comparators; and an audit of the audit: core sufficiency, in-sample and held-out mutation recall, transfer to a second LLM-written corpus.

**Results:** The invariants exposed a ranking defect 353 vignettes had missed. Of 200 audited rules, 92% (95% CI 87.4–95.1) were unresolvable from their locators and ≥7.5% discordant. Only 364 rules (1.1%) ever drove a top-10 differential; of 100 sampled, 6 were concordant. 60.8% (58.7–62.9) of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses ranked top-five (prevalence baseline 51.3%); sepsis prediction failed as pre-specified. Engine and LLM recall@5 did not differ detectably (60.7% vs 59.6%; +1.2 points, 95% CI −2.5 to +5.1; no equivalence margin). SI units left the engine's top five unchanged in 40/40 panels, the LLM's in 8/40; as a verifier it cut the LLM's unsupported top-5 assertions from 9.4% to 5.7% for 0.2 recall points. The 335 curated or quote-verified rules alone kept recall@3/@5 within 0.2 points (@10 −1.2). Static detectors caught 74–100% of structural mutants, ≤13% of plausible wrong thresholds and almost no held-out population-gate or ±20% defects.

**Conclusions:** The methods are cheap, one-command reproducible and here unfavourable: provenance granularity limits auditability, 1% of rules carries the output, static checks miss plausible errors. A traceable engine did not differ detectably from a frontier LLM and can verify it. No clinician audited the rules and no clinical claim is made; code, rules, outputs and a human-audit protocol are public.

**Keywords:** Clinical decision support; Knowledge base; Software verification; Provenance; Laboratory medicine; Large language models

## 1. Introduction

Roughly 70% of clinical decisions draw on laboratory results, yet few reports carry an interpretive comment and post-analytical errors remain a leading source of diagnostic harm [1–3]. Each available remedy has a known weakness. Rule-based interpretation is transparent but narrow and expensive to maintain [4,5]. Supervised models need labelled data and cannot be audited clause by clause [6,7]. Large language models (LLMs) interpret reports fluently, but their answers vary between runs, the data leave the premises, and values are misclassified even when reference ranges are supplied [8–11].

LLMs have changed one part of this picture: a guideline-transcribed rule base that once took a committee years can now be drafted in weeks. This creates a software-engineering problem rather than solving a clinical one. An AI-drafted rule is a hypothesis about what a source says, nobody has read most of them, and the usual validation—authors' vignettes, one institution, no comparator [12]—detects neither mistranscription nor ranking defects. Diagnostic decision-support systems have been compared with GPT-4-class models on 36–201 symptom cases [13–16], formal verification has been applied to guideline knowledge acquisition [17] and external validation of learned models is mature [18], but no method exists for verifying a large AI-drafted laboratory rule base, and no openly licensed, machine-executable rule set for laboratory-panel interpretation was found to compare against (Supplementary Note 5).

This paper treats the rule base as software and asks how it can be verified. We contribute four methods: (i) a property-based test suite of invariants that any correct laboratory-interpretation engine must satisfy, run on randomised panels, as a black box on real panels given identically to the engine and to an LLM, and as a verifier behind the LLM; (ii) a source-concordance protocol, frozen before sampling and reported with explicit denominators, combined with an activity log that identifies which rules actually drive the output; (iii) a hash-frozen, one-shot, multi-dataset benchmark with supervised, prevalence and LLM comparators; and (iv) an audit of the audit, which tests whether the audited core is sufficient, how much the static detectors see in-sample and held-out, and whether the defects recur in a second LLM-written corpus. The tools require only that a rule be expressed as analyte, operator, threshold, unit, population gate and source locator, and are released standalone.

The object of study is Hemoglyph, an offline, single-process engine with 33,183 guideline-transcribed rules, 99.2% of them AI-drafted under a non-clinician first author and none clinically audited. The same engine ships inside a proprietary desktop product owned by the first author; this interest is declared and motivated the frozen design. The paper does not claim that the engine is accurate. It claims that the engine is verifiable and shows what verification found. The clinician audit of rule content is a separate study, outside the scope of this paper, whose protocol and frozen sample are released here so that it can be run independently of the authors (Supplementary Note 19); none of the claims below depends on its result. Only the inference engine, fed with structured analyte–value pairs, is evaluated.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Engine under study

Hemoglyph processes a panel in five stages (Fig. 1): normalisation (UCUM units, aliases, age- and sex-specific reference intervals, 100 derived indices); a rule layer over registry 14.1.0, whose 33,183 executable records each hold a condition on at least one analyte, a population gate, an evidence role (diagnostic criterion, weight 6; supportive or screening, weight 3), a disease identifier and a source locator; an expert syndrome layer of about 900 multi-analyte constellations; fixed-weight fusion (0.35 rule engine, 0.25 Bayesian, 0.20 fuzzy, 0.10 knowledge graph, 0.10 risk models; set by judgement, never tuned on evaluation data), under which the fused score cannot fall below 0.90 × the rule-layer vote; and ranking hygiene (causal attribution, covering-diagnosis suppression, KDIGO chronicity [19], ADA glycaemic criteria [20], UDMI acuity [21]; 25 entries at most). Of the rules, 24,293 (73.2%) are generated two-analyte templates that rank below diseases and cite only a generic standards page; 8,890 attach to 2,502 disease, finding, drug-level or serology entities. No parameter was fitted to any patient record (change log: Supplementary Table S1).

### 2.2 Knowledge-base construction and provenance

For each guideline source the first author prompted a general-purpose LLM with the source text or citation and a fixed JSON schema. Returned records were accepted or rejected by automated audits only—schema validity, analyte present in the 2,045-analyte feature registry, convertible unit, plausible threshold, no duplicate or contradiction, context gate present, locator resolvable—and carry the status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`. The 272 anchor rules were written or line-edited by hand from primary guideline text. No human read the bulk, so we call the registry AI-drafted and automatically audited. Registry 14.1.0 has 33,183 rules and 785 sources.

### 2.3 Verification method 1: executable invariants

A property-based suite of 25 tests generates randomised panels and asserts determinism; unit invariance (SI and conventional units give the same ranking, tolerance 10⁻³); directional monotonicity (a more extreme value never lowers the probability of the finding it defines; 14 analyte–direction pairs); absent-analyte invariance (no syndrome is asserted from an analyte that is not present); and context invariants (documented chronic kidney disease never raises acute kidney injury). It runs on every release with 353 adversarial vignettes and a safety battery, and tests logical consistency, not clinical correctness: a mistranscribed threshold passes every invariant.

Because the same properties can be stated for any reader, four were also run as a *black-box* benchmark in which the engine and the LLM of Section 2.5 received identical real panels: 40 eICU panels in SI units, the same 40 shuffled with five normal distractors, 20 creatinine ladders (×1.0, ×1.5, ×2.0) and 40 panels with the defining analyte of a criterion-met diagnosis removed. Panels, scorer and metrics were committed before any reading and are released as *LabPanel-Check*, a 290-panel conformance package (Supplementary Note 13). Finally, the same criteria served as an *executable verifier* behind the LLM: each entry of the 400 stored blind readings was checked against its own panel as supported, contradicted, analyte-absent or not verifiable; unsupported entries were demoted and recall re-scored, and the engine's own top five passed through the same verifier. A *verified consensus* arm (reciprocal-rank fusion of the engine's ranking with the supported LLM entries) was registered with the rule that recall@5 below 62% would be reported as "not complementary" (Supplementary Note 14). No new LLM call was made.

### 2.4 Verification method 2: source concordance and activity-guided provenance

*Concordance.* The protocol was frozen before sampling (seed 20261026). We drew 100 of the 272 curated anchors and 100 executable candidates (50 relevant to the ICU label set, 50 uniform from the rest) and compared analyte, operator and threshold with unit, population qualifier and disease attribution against the source reached from the registry locator, with Crossref title and PubMed abstract as fall-back for paywalled DOIs. Labels were CONCORDANT, DISCORDANT or UNRESOLVABLE, with the tie-break "when in doubt, unresolvable"; an unresolvable rule is never counted as concordant. Rates are reported with two denominators (resolvable; all sampled) and Wilson 95% CIs. The rater was an AI agent working under the first author's direction and is declared as such.

*Activity.* A second protocol, frozen with registered expectations, logged for every rule on 3,814 real panels (the 2,314 eICU stays and 1,500 NHANES 2015–18 adults; seed 20261027) whether it fired and whether its disease entered the top-10 differential; rules that contributed at least once form the *active core*. Each active rule's locator was searched automatically for its threshold, and 100 active rules outside the concordance sample were drawn (seed 20261028) and labelled by the same rater, this time with a verbatim quote required for every concordant or discordant verdict. No rule or locator was changed.

### 2.5 Verification method 3: frozen one-shot benchmark

An internal statistical analysis plan (Supplementary Note 1; 15 dated amendments) fixed metrics, label maps, thresholds and comparators; it was hash-frozen in the repository but not publicly registered, so we write "pre-specified", not "pre-registered". Before each of two quarantine rounds, code and registry were frozen with SHA-256 manifests and the quarantine data were run once. Round 1 failed two superiority criteria because a guideline-correctness change lowered CKD recall; the criteria were re-specified as non-inferiority before any round-2 file was opened, and round 2 met them, so round 2 is evidence of non-regression rather than the confirmatory test originally planned (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S2). Every number in Tables 1, 3–5 and 7 and Figures 2 and 4–8 comes from frozen engine v7 with registry 14.1.0; the black-box test (Table 6) and the audit study (Section 3.8) used the later engine v8.2 and registries 15.1.0–15.3.0, which change no v7 number (Supplementary Table S17).

*Datasets and reference standards* (Table 2; Supplementary Table S3). eICU-CRD demo v2.0.1 [22]: 2,314 adult stays with at least eight analytes in the first panel, the clinician problem list mapped to 31 groups as reference (mapping audit on 400 stays: precision 100%, sensitivity 98.3%). MIMIC-IV demo [23]: 235 admissions, ICD-10. NHANES 2013–14, 2015–16 and 2017–18: 3,000 randomly sampled adults per cycle, referenced to ADA, KDIGO, WHO and ACG criteria and to self-report. PhysioNet/CinC 2019 [24]: sets A, B and B2 (14,420 patients), Sepsis-3 onset. UCI thyroid0387 [25], HCV [26], ILPD [27] and 50 PMC case reports [28].

*Metrics and comparators.* The primary metric is flat recall@k of clinician-listed diagnoses (k = 3, 5, 10); secondary metrics are two-tier recall, panel-supported recall, per-group AUROC, the false-assertion rate in all-normal adults, sepsis AUROC, calibration [29] and a component ablation. Comparators were a raw key-analyte threshold; gradient boosting and logistic regression on the same analytes (five-fold out-of-fold); a prevalence-ranking baseline; and an unnamed frontier LLM run by the authors through the Arena.ai agent platform (2026-10-21 to 2026-10-25; n = 400), which routes among several frontier model families without exposing model or version, so "unnamed frontier LLM" denotes a capability tier, not a verified identity. The LLM received the identical panel, age and sex with a fixed prompt requesting ten ranked diagnoses, blind to labels; 40 panels were re-read for test–retest; scoring used the engine's label map and script (Supplementary Note 2).

*Statistics.* Labels are nested in stays (ICC 0.04), so eICU proportions and paired differences use a stay-cluster bootstrap (4,000 resamples) and the cluster-adjusted McNemar test [30,31]; Wilson intervals elsewhere; DeLong or bootstrap intervals for AUROC; Benjamini–Hochberg correction across per-group tests. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI [32], adapted for a system with no fitted parameters.

### 2.6 Verification method 4: verifying the verification

Three questions about the methods themselves were pre-specified, with protocol and detector code frozen before any result, and run on the post-audit registry 15.1.0. *Sufficiency*: an arm in which only 335 rules remain executable—284 curated rules, 19 further rules with a verbatim source quote and 32 reference-interval conventions—was run on the same cohorts and seeds as the full registry; "not load-bearing" (|Δ| ≤ 0.5 points) and "partially load-bearing" (0.5–3 points) were defined in advance. *Mutation*: seven static defect detectors, calibrated on 15.1.0 and then frozen, were scored for recall on eight classes of synthetic defect injected into 303 seed rules and for specificity on the unmutated seeds. Because that estimate is in-sample, a held-out test with eight further defect families was pre-registered afterwards, with its expected blind spots stated before the run, on 300 AI-drafted rules of registry 15.3.0 and on the external corpus below (Supplementary Note 20). *Transfer*: the detectors were run unchanged on registry 14.1.0 and on a second LLM-written rule set (200 entities × 2 rules, fixed prompt, no templates, hash-frozen before the run), written by the same agent that wrote the detectors; the test therefore asks whether the defect classes recur under a different generation process, not whether they are independent.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Executable invariants

All 25 tests pass on the current engine. On the frozen v7 the suite exposed one defect that the 353 adversarial vignettes had not: a well-supported renal finding was capped beneath a presumptive explainer scoring below 0.80, so the leading renal probability *fell* as creatinine rose from 1.6 to 2.2 mg/dL. The fix applies an existing rule uniformly and passed the full gate (eICU recall@3/5/10 48.4/61.0/74.1% versus 47.9/60.8/74.1% for v7); the frozen v7 numbers reported below are unchanged. The engine is deterministic (byte-identical output in 50/50 repeated stays) and runs on one CPU core in about 1 s per panel without network calls.

### 3.2 Source concordance

Of 200 rules sampled under the frozen protocol (Fig. 3b), only 16 could be resolved from the locators the registry provides. Fifteen were discordant—7 attribution errors among curated anchors (a hypernatraemia rule citing the hyponatraemia guideline, for example) and 8 thresholds or definitions absent from an on-topic source (a candidate acute-kidney-injury "criterion" defined by a fixed creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL and attributed to KDIGO 2012, which defines AKI only by change from baseline)—and one was concordant. The remaining 184 (92%, 95% CI 87.4–95.1) were unresolvable: 12 with no retrievable text, 55 with only a title or abstract, 46 citing generic methodological standards that cannot contain a disease-specific threshold, and 71 organisation landing pages. Retrievability and verifiability proved to be different properties (Supplementary Table S18): a source opened to at least 500 characters of text for 94.0% (89.8–96.5) of sampled rules, but the encoded statement was found in it for only 8.0% (5.0–12.6). Because an attribution error is visible from a title while concordance needs the full text, 15/16 is not an estimate of the discordance rate. The defensible statements are that at least 7.5% (4.6–12.0) of sampled rules are demonstrably discordant and that the registry cannot be audited rule by rule from its own locators. The audit is single-rater, by a declared AI agent; no inter-rater reliability is claimed.

### 3.3 Activity-guided provenance audit

On 3,814 real panels, 1,991 rules fired, 364 (1.1%) ever placed a diagnosis in a top-10 differential, 174 did so ten or more times, and 31,192 (94.0%) never fired (Fig. 3a). The active core holds 46 of the 272 curated anchors and 105 generated templates. Automated search located a rule's threshold in a document reached from its locator for 8 of the 364 active rules. In the fresh sample of 100 active rules, 6 were concordant (ADA 2026 diabetes and prediabetes criteria, ADA/EASD 2024 DKA severity and HHS criteria, BSG 2021 iron-deficiency anaemia), 2 discordant (an HbA1c "> 5.7%" pattern against an inclusive 5.7–6.4% range; CK "≥ 1000" against "> 1000 U/L") and 92 unresolvable (85.0–95.9). None of the four registered expectations was met (Supplementary Note 15).

### 3.4 Intensive care (eICU-CRD demo)

In 2,314 adult ICU stays from 186 US hospitals, using the first panel alone, 47.9% (stay-clustered 95% CI 45.8–50.0) of 2,432 clinician-listed, laboratory-relevant diagnoses appeared in the top 3, 60.8% (58.7–62.9) in the top 5 and 74.1% (72.2–76.0) in the top 10 (Table 3). Precision@5 was 14.2% (13.3–15.2), F1@5 23.1% and NDCG@5 56.5% (54.1–58.8) against an incomplete problem list. Recall@5 ranged from 88.1% for acute myocardial infarction and 83.0% for anaemia down to 49.1% for sepsis and 35.8% for CKD. A prevalence-ranking baseline reached recall@5 51.3% and NDCG@5 39.0%, and overtook the engine at k = 10 (76.8% vs 74.1%): the engine's contribution is ordering, not coverage (Supplementary Table S11). Of the 10,380 top-5 assertions, 14.2% were on the problem list, 54.8% were uncoded but criterion-positive in the same panel, 12.6% were criterion-negative (the closest available estimate of false positives) and 18.4% fell in groups without a single-analyte criterion.

The reference standard bounds these numbers (Fig. 2b): of 2,707 label targets, 541 belonged to groups with no single laboratory criterion, 275 lacked the key analyte and 431 were contradicted by the first panel. Among the 1,460 labels whose first panel met the guideline criterion (a secondary metric defined after the ceiling was observed), flat recall was 29.6/62.5/78.0/91.8% at k = 1/3/5/10. Supervised models trained on the labels (Fig. 4) exceeded the untrained engine only in outcome-defined groups without a laboratory signature (sepsis AUROC 0.82 vs 0.68; gastrointestinal bleeding 0.85 vs 0.75; alcohol withdrawal 0.80 vs 0.54) and were matched or beaten in nine guideline-defined groups.

### 3.5 Ablation, community survey and external cohorts

Two components carry the signal (Table 1): removing the rule-engine vote cost 12.1 points of flat recall@5 (−14.4 to −9.8) and removing the syndrome layer 9.5 points (−11.1 to −7.9); the Bayesian network contributed 1.3 points and any fusion-weight profile changed recall by at most 1.4 points. Restricting the registry to the 272 curated anchors lowered recall@5 by 0.9 points (−2.1 to +0.2), so the headline rests on hand-curated components and registry scale is not claimed as a merit. In 3,000 NHANES adults per cycle, AUROCs reproduced across the development cycle (2017–18) and the two quarantine cycles (2013–14, 2015–16): diabetes 0.946/0.938/0.924, CKD 0.959/0.968/0.962, anaemia 0.972/0.994/0.983 (Fig. 5; Table 4); against self-reported physician diagnosis, which does not overlap the inputs, AUROC was 0.90–0.92 for diabetes and 0.75–0.82 for kidney disease, and among guideline-healthy adults the engine asserted a disease in 2 of 485 (0.4%). In PhysioNet/CinC 2019 the sepsis score discriminated Sepsis-3 onset with AUROC 0.600, 0.610 and 0.588 in sets A, B and B2, against 0.691, 0.712 and 0.679 for a supervised logistic model on the same features (Fig. 6)—a pre-specified negative result that was not tuned away. External cohorts, calibration (Fig. 8) and subgroups are in Table 4 and Supplementary Note 17.

### 3.6 Blinded comparison with an unnamed frontier LLM

On 400 stays (291 with scorable labels; 596 labels), recall@5—the pre-specified primary comparison—was 60.7% (56.5–65.0) for Hemoglyph and 59.6% (55.3–63.6) for the LLM, a difference of +1.2 points (−2.5 to +5.1; cluster-adjusted McNemar p = 0.54). The LLM was better at rank 1 (28.2% vs 24.0%, p = 0.013) and rank 3 (50.8% vs 47.0%, p = 0.041); the engine was better at rank 10 (72.0% vs 65.1%, p = 0.0002) and left fewer labels absent altogether (21.8% vs 34.9%); mean reciprocal rank was 0.391 vs 0.408 (Fig. 7; Table 5). On 40 panels re-read by the LLM, top-5 agreement with its own first reading was κ 0.69; the engine's was 1.00.

### 3.7 Black-box conformance and the engine as a verifier behind the LLM

*Conformance* (Table 6). SI units left the engine's top five identical in 40/40 panels (Jaccard 1.00) and the LLM's in 8/40 (0.72); shuffled order with distractors, 35/40 (0.96) against 10/40 (0.73); the LLM figures include its own test–retest variability. The first engine run gave 36/40 on SI units, and all four discrepancies traced to one defect the in-process suite had not reached—a record mirrored under a legacy identifier kept its reported unit, so a normal bilirubin of 9 µmol/L met an 8 mg/dL panic threshold. The fix has a regression test; the frozen v7 numbers are untouched. The engine violated creatinine monotonicity in 2 of 20 ladders at ×2.0 (logged, not changed). After removal of the defining analyte, the target remained at rank ≤ 5 in 3/40 engine and 14/40 LLM panels, and in a blinded NHANES test the engine asserted no disease in 40 guideline-healthy adults while the LLM did so in four (Supplementary Note 13).

*Verifier* (Table 7). Of 2,424 LLM entries, 1,636 mapped to a group with a criterion (1,439 supported, 182 contradicted, 15 analyte-absent) and 788 were not verifiable. Among mapped top-5 entries, 140 of 1,486 (9.4%) were unsupported (registered expectation 15–30%); the engine's own top five failed the same proxy criteria in 233 of 1,518 (15.3%; registered 5–15%), mostly where its hyperglycaemia and prerenal rules are more permissive than the criterion, but never without the analyte present (0/1,518). Demoting unsupported LLM entries left recall@5 essentially unchanged (59.6% → 59.4%; paired −0.2, 95% CI −0.5 to 0.0), raised recall@1 by 1.2 points (0.2–2.3; p = 0.014) and reduced the unsupported share to 5.7%; of 197 demoted entries, 10 were clinician labels (5.1%; registered ≤ 10%). The parameter-free fusion gave recall@1/5/10 of 21.8/61.2/76.3%—below the registered 62% at rank 5, better only at rank 10 (+4.5, CI 2.2–6.8) and worse on outcome-type labels (51.9% → 42.7%), because the filter admits only what is verifiable. Per protocol the systems are reported as not complementary; no other rule was tried (Supplementary Note 14).

### 3.8 Verifying the verification

*Sufficiency.* With only the 335 curated, quote-verified or convention rules executable, eICU flat recall@3/5/10 was 47.8/61.2/73.1% against 48.0/61.2/74.3% for the full registry 15.1.0, two-tier recall@1/3/5 was 32.3/60.9/70.1% against 33.3/62.3/72.4%, mean per-label AUROC 0.761 against 0.766, NHANES AUROCs were identical and healthy-adult false assertions 0/51 in both arms. Paired differences were significant only at @10 (p = 0.005), driven by diabetes and renal pattern templates: under the pre-stated thresholds the other 30,735 executable rules are not load-bearing at @3 and @5 and partially load-bearing at @10.

*Mutation.* In-sample detector recall (Fig. 3c) was 1.00 for inverted or impossible tiers (301/301), 0.98 for multi-analyte rules collapsed to a single ubiquitous analyte (138/141), 0.74 for unit substitution (113/152), 0.55 for thresholds pushed outside physiological limits (40/73), 0.46 for random source substitution (40/88), 0.13 for operator flips (36/274) and 0.003 for plausible threshold changes (1/303); specificity on unmutated seeds was 0.974, and four of the eight flagged seeds were real defects. These, with 126 unit-label and 14 source errors found during calibration, were corrected in registry 15.2.0 under a written protocol with every gate count identical before and after. The held-out test (Supplementary Note 20) showed where the in-sample figures hold and where they do not: unit-swap recall held at 0.81 on the 300 AI-drafted rules and 0.70 on the external corpus, but operator-flip recall fell to 0.035 on the external corpus, whose two-rules-per-entity structure gives the direction detector nothing to compare against; decimal-place errors were caught in 13–16%; a benign, consistent unit conversion was never flagged; and, as pre-stated, removing a population gate, swapping in a same-domain source, encoding a contradictory conjunction or shifting a threshold by 20% went undetected in at least 98% of cases.

*Transfer and ledger.* Per 100 rules, any-detector density was 23.6 (95% CI 22.8–24.5) in registry 14.1.0, 1.3 (1.0–1.6) in 15.1.0 and 12.8 (9.8–16.4) in the second LLM-written corpus, where 37 of 51 flags were true defects (Supplementary Note 16). A per-rule verification ledger (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Note 18) joins the four methods for the 605 curated, active-core or sufficiency-arm rules: of the 272 anchors, 18 are quote-verified, 8 discordant and 141 unresolvable; of the 364 active-core rules, 145 were retired, 37 are quote-verified and 157 unresolvable. It also exposed seven discordant rules, five open detector flags and one unimplemented protocol line left unchanged, all closed under a written protocol in registry 15.3.0 with the non-regression gate unchanged.

*Reproduction.* From a clean clone of the repository, one command rebuilt engine v7 from its git tag, checked it against the freeze manifests, re-ran the tests, the ledger, the mutation table and the frozen engine on NHANES and eICU, and reproduced every number in Tables 3 and 4 exactly (Supplementary Note 21). The first attempt failed because two inputs of the ledger existed only in the authors' working copy; they were committed and the run repeated.

## 4. Discussion

Each method produced information that conventional validation had not: two engine defects that 353 adversarial vignettes had missed; a rule base that cannot be audited from its own locators, with at least 7.5% of sampled rules discordant; an output carried by 1.1% of the rules, which fail in the same way as the bulk; recall@5 not detectably different from a frontier LLM while a prevalence baseline reached 51%, so that the engine's contribution is ordering (NDCG@5 56.5% vs 39.0%) and specificity rather than coverage; and an audited core of 335 rules that preserves recall@3 and @5 within 0.2 points at a cost of 1.2 points at @10.

Executable invariants are a genuine advantage of a rule engine over an LLM. They can be stated, run on every release and violated only visibly. On identical panels the engine held unit and presentation invariance where the LLM's output changed in most panels, and the same test found two engine defects: a violated invariant is a bug report with a reproducer, whereas an LLM's changed answer is not actionable. The criteria also work as a verifier *behind* the LLM, reducing its unsupported top-5 assertions from 9.4% to 5.7% for a change in recall@5 of −0.2 points; fusing the two systems did not help at rank 5, because filtering admits only the verifiable while the engine's residual advantage lies in outcome syndromes that no single criterion verifies. Invariants establish consistency, not correctness, and the held-out mutation test bounds how little else the static layer sees: a wrong but plausible number, a missing population gate or a wrong-but-on-topic citation passes it.

The concordance audit shows why correctness is hard to reach. The limiting factor was locator granularity, not rater effort: organisation-level URLs and generic standards make 92% of rules uncheckable, and where checking was possible the error was usually attribution. Audit protocols should therefore require the verbatim source sentence for every verdict, registries should carry section- or table-level locators, and the clinician audit that follows is designed to be run by others; its protocol—50 active-core and 50 anchor rules, two blinded clinical raters, verbatim-quote verdicts, Cohen's κ, sample size and stopping rule—is fixed in Supplementary Note 19 before any rater is recruited.

The benchmark numbers measure the reference standard at least as much as the engine: a quarter of problem-list entries were not inferable from the first panel, the panel-supported subset is recovered at 78.0% (k = 5) and 91.8% (k = 10), and coded precision (14.2%) is a lower bound because 54.8% of uncoded top-5 assertions satisfy the group's criterion in the same panel. Where the engine loses—sepsis, bleeding, withdrawal, CKD without a second creatinine—the label is an outcome learned from hidden correlates or needs longitudinal data, and part of the loss was chosen: withdrawing single-creatinine CKD assertions to comply with KDIGO cost three points of recall@5.

Ablation and sufficiency agree that the result lives in the rule vote and a few hundred curated constellations. The bulk adds one to three points and 94% of it never fired, so the activity log converts an unauditable registry into a tractable verification queue: a release could ship only the 335 verified rules and carry the remaining 30,735 as labelled, off-by-default hypotheses until each is audited, at a measured cost of at most 0.2 points at @3 and @5 and 1.2 points at @10. The design answers the criticisms made of recent LLM benchmarks—unstable metrics, leakage and post-hoc selection [28,33]: releases were frozen with manifests, quarantine data were run once, the failed round is disclosed, the whole chain reproduces from a clean clone, and the LLM's own test–retest variability (κ 0.69) exceeded the between-system difference. Unlike the Isabel and DXplain studies [13–16,34], this study isolates laboratory-only reasoning on more than 20,000 encounters and releases its knowledge base under an open licence; its per-entry rule identifier and source are consistent with the transparency principle of the FDA's January 2026 clinical-decision-support guidance, without any regulatory claim.

### 4.1 Limitations

No clinician audited the rules or adjudicated outputs. Both concordance audits are single-rater, by a declared AI agent, without inter-rater reliability; the concordance figures are therefore bounds on auditability under a stated rater and protocol, not estimates of clinical correctness, which is the object of the separate study in Supplementary Note 19. The LLM comparison tested superiority, not equivalence (p = 0.54 does not establish parity); it is an author-run self-baseline through a platform that does not disclose the model, without retrieval augmentation or clinician adjudication, on a public dataset the model may have seen in training. Nearly three quarters of the executable rules are generated two-analyte templates citing a generic standards page, and "dormant" means dormant on the analyte coverage of the two corpora used. The components that carry the result were written by a non-clinician first author; 34 sources lack a locator; Bayesian priors are hand-coded without a per-value citation table; no version-control history exists for the development period. Only the open demo subsets of eICU and MIMIC-IV were used, with problem lists and ICD codes as reference standards; quarantine criteria were weakened after round 1; the panel-supported metric is post hoc; NHANES laboratory-defined labels overlap the inputs; raw scores are uncalibrated; external cohorts are small; the OCR front end, Iranian populations and non-English laboratory conventions were not evaluated. The verifier's criteria are proxies, so its verdicts bound rather than measure unsupported assertion. The static detectors were calibrated on the registry they then scored and the second LLM-written corpus was written by the same agent; the held-out test bounds the resulting optimism for two defect families and confirms blindness to four others. The first author is a secondary-school student with a financial interest in the commercial product, and the system, including 99.2% of the rules, was developed with AI-agent assistance. Hemoglyph is a research prototype with no regulatory clearance.

## 5. Conclusion

Executable invariants, a frozen source-concordance protocol with an activity log, a hash-frozen one-shot benchmark and an audit of the audit are four low-cost, reproducible methods for verifying an AI-drafted clinical rule base. A rule engine whose every output carries a rule identifier, threshold and source can be audited in ways a language model cannot, and can audit the language model's output in turn. Applied to Hemoglyph, they found defects that vignettes had missed, showed that provenance granularity rather than rule count limits auditability, that 1.1% of the rules carry the output and 335 curated or quote-verified rules alone preserve recall@5, that static checks cannot catch a plausible wrong number, and that an untrained, traceable engine performs within the ceiling the reference standard allows and not detectably differently from a frontier LLM. The limits—an unaudited rule base, outcome-defined syndromes, uncalibrated scores—are stated so that they can be measured and closed.

### Supplementary Note 12 — Full-length Methods and Results text of revision R5 (verbatim; condensed in the main text for length — superseded where it differs from the main text)

#### R5 Methods

##### 2.1 Engine

Hemoglyph (`analyse_gidlines.py` [sic—historical file name], v7) processes a panel in five stages: normalisation (UCUM units, aliases, age/sex reference intervals, 100 derived indices); a rule layer over registry 14.1.0 (33,183 executable leaves, where a leaf is one registry record with a machine-evaluable condition on at least one analyte, a disease identifier and an `executable: true` flag—retired, template and empty-expression records are kept for provenance but never evaluated—a count that moved from 34,005 in release 13.1.0 through 33,230 (13.3.0), 33,170 (13.4.2) and 33,178 (14.0.0) as 1,307 rules were retired and 485 guideline-sourced rules were added, Supplementary Table S10; each with analyte conditions, population gates, evidence role—diagnostic criterion weight 6, supportive or screening weight 3—disease identifier and source); an expert syndrome layer (~900 constellations with anchor strengths 0.95/0.88/0.80/0.72/0.45, operators absent/between/neg/present and context predicates for pregnancy, age, sex, medication, condition, family history and encounter); fixed-weight fusion (weights 0.35 rule engine, 0.25 Bayesian, 0.20 fuzzy, 0.10 knowledge graph, 0.10 risk models, set by the first author as a judgement that explicit criteria should dominate, with four context profiles, e.g. toxicology 0.55 for rules; no weight was derived from or tuned on any evaluation dataset, and Table 1 reports the sensitivity to them) with an expert floor (the fused score of a diagnosis cannot fall below 0.90 × the rule-layer vote, so that the four other layers can modulate but never veto a guideline rule). The Bayesian layer has 75 disease nodes with hand-coded prevalence priors (constants or age bands, e.g. type 2 diabetes 0.08, CKD 0.10) and hand-coded likelihood ratios for 68 of them, taken from diagnostic-accuracy literature cited in code comments (e.g. lipase ≥ 3 × ULN, LR+ 18 for acute pancreatitis); none is estimated from data, so the engine remains untrained in the sense that no parameter was fitted to any patient record; and ranking hygiene (causal attribution via a disease–finding explanation graph, covering-diagnosis suppression of explained findings, KDIGO chronicity [17], ADA glycaemic criteria [18], UDMI acuity [19], maximum 25 entries). Engine changes between releases (C1–C16) are listed with dates in Supplementary Table S1; none was fitted to outcome data and no rule was written for a specific missed case. This is supported by the dated change log and by the fact that every change is expressed as a guideline citation plus a synthetic vignette rather than a parameter value; no version-control history exists for the development period, so the statement cannot be audited by commit diff and is offered as a documented process claim, not a verified one.

##### 2.2 Knowledge base construction and provenance

'AI assistance' means the following. For each guideline source the first author prompted a general-purpose LLM with the source text or citation and a fixed JSON schema; the model returned candidate rule records (analyte, operator, threshold, unit, population gate, evidence role, disease identifier, source locator). These records were not individually edited by a human: they were accepted or rejected by automated audits (schema validity, analyte exists in the 2,045-analyte feature registry, unit convertible, threshold within physiological range, no duplicate or contradictory record, context gate present where the guideline requires one, source locator resolvable) and carry the status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`. The 272 anchor rules were written or line-edited by hand by the first author from the primary guideline text and carry higher weight; the 13 demoted anchors and all rules retired between releases were removed by rule-level review of defect reports, not by outcome data. No record of the proportion of candidate rules that a human read exists, and we therefore describe the bulk as AI-drafted and automatically audited, not human-reviewed. Sources: KDIGO, ADA, WHO, ACC/AHA, EASL, ATA, ESC, CLSI, Tietz and others listed in the registry. Source locators were verified with `registry_source_verify.py` (Crossref resolution, HTTP reachability). Counts by release: 13.3.0, 33,230 rules/747 sources; 13.4.2, 33,170/781; 14.1.0, 33,183/785.

##### 2.3 Pre-specification, freezes and quarantine

The SAP (Supplementary Note 1) fixed metrics, label maps, thresholds and comparators; 15 amendments are dated and justified. Freezes v14 and v15 recorded SHA-256 manifests. Quarantine round 1 (MIMIC-IV demo; NHANES 2013–14; sepsis set B) used superiority criteria and failed; round 2 (NHANES 2015–16; sepsis set B2; healthy sweep with a new seed) used non-inferiority criteria specified before execution and passed. Two data-loader defects discovered during round 2 (string patient identifiers; a dropped derived NHANES variable) were fixed before scoring without touching the engine and are documented.

Data sources, reference standards and their roles are summarised in Table 2. eICU-CRD demo v2.0.1 [20]: 2,520 stays → 2,512 adults → 2,314 with ≥ 8 analytes in the first panel; reference = clinician problem list mapped by regular expressions to 31 groups (audit on 400 stays: precision 100%, sensitivity 98.3%; per-group values in Supplementary Table S9). MIMIC-IV demo [21]: 235 admissions, ICD-10 reference. UCI thyroid0387 [23], UCI HCV [24], ILPD [25], PMC-Patients 50 case reports [26]. Participant flow in Supplementary Table S3.

##### 2.5 Metrics

Primary: flat recall@k of clinician-listed diagnoses (k = 3, 5, 10). Secondary: two-tier recall; panel-supported recall (labels whose first panel met a guideline criterion fixed in `panel_supported_metric.py` before scoring; groups without a single criterion excluded); per-group AUROC of the group score. Screening: AUROC and false-assertion rate in all-normal adults. Sepsis: AUROC of the sepsis-group score. Calibration: calibration slope and intercept, ECE (10 equal-frequency bins), Brier with cluster-bootstrap CI, log-loss and decision curves before and after cross-fitted isotonic regression [27]. Ablation: the frozen engine with one component disabled or altered per arm (each of five fusion layers, syndrome layer, registry, anchor-only registry, `unverified_source` rules removed, fusion-weight and shrinkage settings), scored on the same 2,314 stays.

##### 2.6 Comparators

B1, raw key-analyte threshold; B2, gradient boosting and logistic regression trained on the same analytes, five-fold out-of-fold; registry-off ablation; LLM: an unnamed frontier model run by the authors through a chat interface on 2026-10-21 (n = 60), 2026-10-22 (n = 100) and 2026-10-23 (n = 240), re-read 2026-10-24 and alternative prompt 2026-10-25; the interface exposes no temperature or sampling parameters, so these are the provider defaults; the LLM was accessed through the Arena.ai agent platform, which routes requests among several frontier model families (including GPT, Claude and Gemini) and does not expose the specific model or version to the user; 'unnamed frontier LLM' therefore denotes a capability tier, not a verified identity, and the comparison cannot be reproduced against a named model version—a limitation stated in the Discussion; blinded to labels, with a fixed prompt requesting ten ranked diagnoses from the identical panel, age and sex; 40 panels re-read for test–retest and read with an alternative prompt; scoring against the frozen engine v7 by the same label map and script. Prompts, outputs and the full protocol are released (Supplementary Note 2).

After the freeze, twelve review-driven improvement proposals were tested against the frozen artefacts before any code was changed; the procedure, the four supported and eight refuted proposals, and the resulting development version v8 are described in Supplementary Note 9. No frozen number in this paper derives from v8.

##### 2.8 Source-concordance audit

Protocol frozen before sampling (`eval/source_audit/PROTOCOL.md`). Seed 20261026; 100 of 272 curated anchors and 100 executable candidates (50 "eICU-relevant": feature present in ≥ 50% of eICU first panels and disease in an evaluated label group; 50 uniform from the rest), registry 14.1.0. Fields compared: analyte, operator/threshold with unit, population qualifier, disease attribution. Labels CONCORDANT / DISCORDANT / UNRESOLVABLE with the tie-break "when in doubt, unresolvable"; unresolvable is never counted as concordant. Sources were retrieved by the registry locator (curl, 20 s), with Crossref title and PubMed abstract as fall-back for paywalled DOIs and direct PDF retrieval for KDIGO guidelines. Rates are reported with two denominators (resolvable and all sampled) and Wilson 95% CIs. The rater was an AI agent under the first author's supervision (declared); the protocol's second-rater arm did not yield a valid dataset under the quotation rule and no inter-rater reliability is claimed.

##### 2.9 Statistics and software

Labels are nested in stays (ICC 0.04); eICU proportions and paired differences use stay-cluster bootstrap (4,000 resamples) and the cluster-adjusted McNemar test [28,29]; Wilson intervals elsewhere; 1,000-sample bootstrap or DeLong for AUROC; NHANES AUROCs additionally weighted by examination weights; Benjamini–Hochberg across per-group tests. Python 3.11/3.13, numpy, pandas, scikit-learn. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI [30], adapted for a system that is not fitted to data (model-training items answered "not applicable" with justification), with the DECIDE-AI early-stage items consulted for the Limitations; the completed checklist is Supplementary Note 5.7.

#### R5 Results

##### 3.1 System and knowledge base

The architecture (Fig. 1; Section 2.1) was frozen as engine v7 with registry 14.1.0 (33,183 executable rules: 272 hand-curated anchors and 32,911 AI-drafted, unaudited candidates from 785 guideline sources; ~900 curated syndrome constellations). The engine is deterministic (byte-identical output in 50/50 repeated stays), runs on one CPU core in ~1 s per panel and makes no network calls. Open and proprietary components are tabulated in Supplementary Table S15; only the open components were evaluated.

The registry was constructed with AI assistance from guideline text and filtered by automated consistency audits; it has not been audited rule-by-rule by clinicians, so the per-rule error rate is unknown. Each output entry displays its rule's status and evidence tier as machine-readable fields. A provenance audit resolved 112 DOIs via Crossref and reached 533 landing pages; 39 sources were unreachable from our sandbox, 34 lacked a locator, nine invalid DOIs were removed and 28 rules were flagged `unverified_source` (Supplementary Table S10, Fig. S1).

##### 3.2 Pre-specification, freezes and quarantine

Evaluation followed an internal statistical analysis plan with 15 dated amendments (Supplementary Note 1), time-stamped and hash-frozen inside the repository but not publicly registered; we therefore write 'pre-specified', not 'pre-registered'. Before each quarantine round, code and registry were frozen with a SHA-256 manifest, and quarantine data were run once (the MIMIC-IV demo re-run with v7 is the labelled exception). Round 1 (MIMIC-IV demo, NHANES 2013–14, PhysioNet sepsis set B) failed two primary criteria written as superiority over the prior release, because guideline-correctness changes (a single creatinine no longer asserts chronic kidney disease) lowered CKD label recall. We report that failure, re-specified the criteria as non-inferiority (Amendment 15, dated after Round 1 and before any Round-2 file was opened: NHANES AUROC within 0.03 on six targets with ≤1% healthy false assertions; sepsis AUROC not more than 0.02 below; no new safety-battery failure), implemented six further correctness changes (v7) and ran Round 2 (NHANES 2015–16, sepsis set B2, new healthy sweep), which met them (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S2). Because the criteria changed between rounds, Round 2 is supportive evidence of non-regression, not the confirmatory test originally planned. All results below are for frozen v7/14.1.0 unless stated.

##### 3.3 Intensive care (eICU-CRD demo)

In 2,314 adult ICU stays from 186 US hospitals, using the first panel with ≥8 analytes and no clinical text, 47.9% (stay-clustered 95% CI 45.8–50.0) of 2,432 clinician-listed, laboratory-relevant diagnoses appeared in the top-3, 60.8% (58.7–62.9) in the top-5 and 74.1% (72.2–76.0) in the top-10 of the flat differential (Table 4); a further 275 coded labels lacked the key analyte and are reported as 'analyte missing'. Raising the analyte threshold to ≥25 changed top-5 recall by ≤1 point. Counting either tier, top-5 recall was 71.9%. Precision@5 was 14.2% (13.3–15.2), F1@5 23.1% and NDCG@5 56.5% (54.1–58.8) against an incomplete problem list (lower bounds). Recall@5 was 88.1% for acute myocardial infarction, 82.8% for heart failure, 83.0% for anaemia and 77.5% for acute kidney injury, but 49.1% for sepsis, 45.1% for diabetes and 35.8% for CKD.

A prevalence-ranking baseline (most frequent groups whose key analyte is present) reached recall@5 51.3% and NDCG@5 39.0%; at k = 10 its recall (76.8%) exceeded the engine's 74.1%, so the engine's advantage is ranking, not coverage (Supplementary Table S11). Automated laboratory-criterion matching of the 10,380 top-5 assertions (fixed single-analyte criteria written before scoring, not clinician adjudication) found 14.2% on the problem list, 54.8% not coded but satisfying the group's laboratory criterion in the same panel, 12.6% criterion-negative—the closest available estimate of true false positives—and 18.4% in groups without a single-analyte criterion. Precision@5 therefore lies between 14.2% and 69.0%; whether the uncoded criterion-positive findings were clinically useful cannot be judged without a clinician.

The reference standard bounds these metrics (Fig. 2b). Of 2,707 label targets, 541 belonged to groups with no single laboratory criterion, 275 lacked the key analyte and 431 were contradicted by the first panel (e.g. hypokalaemia coded with potassium 4.1 mmol/L after repletion). Among the 1,460 labels whose first panel met the guideline criterion—a secondary metric whose thresholds were fixed before scoring but whose definition followed observation of the ceiling—flat recall was 29.6/62.5/78.0/91.8% at k = 1/3/5/10 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Three comparators used identical inputs (Fig. 4). A raw key-analyte threshold matched or exceeded the engine's AUROC for single-analyte groups (hyperkalaemia 0.96 vs 0.92; anaemia 0.83 vs 0.78), as expected for findings that are definitions. Gradient boosting and logistic regression trained on the labels (five-fold out-of-fold) exceeded the untrained engine only in outcome-defined groups without a laboratory signature—sepsis (0.82 vs 0.68), GI bleeding (0.85 vs 0.75), alcohol withdrawal (0.80 vs 0.54)—and were matched or beaten in nine guideline-defined groups (CKD 0.79 vs 0.81; hyponatraemia 0.85 vs 0.89; cirrhosis 0.83 vs 0.87). Adding documented history and medications raised recall@5 by 0.9 points for labels not named in the history (n = 2,467) but by 23.3 points for labels already named (n = 240); the context arm mainly echoes documentation and is reported only in the Supplement.

##### 3.4 Component ablation

Two components carry the signal (Table 1; Supplementary Table S12). Removing the rule-engine vote cost 12.1 points of flat recall@5 (−14.4 to −9.8) and removing the expert syndrome layer cost 9.5 points (−11.1 to −7.9). The Bayesian network contributed 1.3 points; the fuzzy, knowledge-graph and risk-model layers and the fusion refinements each lay within ±0.5 points; uniform fusion weights cost 1.4 points and rule-heavy or rule-light weights changed recall by ≤1.1 points, so the hand-set weights are not a hidden tuning knob. Restricting the registry to the 272 curated anchors (32,911 AI-drafted rules off) lowered flat recall@5 by 0.9 points (−2.1 to +0.2) with the syndrome layer on and by 3.1 points with it off; the syndrome layer contributed 9.5 points with the full registry and 11.7 with anchors only. The AI-drafted bulk is therefore partly redundant with the syndrome layer rather than inert, but the headline rests on the curated anchors and syndrome constellations—the components not yet independently audited—and registry scale is not claimed as a merit.

##### 3.5 Community survey: three independent NHANES cycles

In 3,000 adults per cycle, unweighted AUROCs reproduced across the development cycle (2017–18) and the two quarantine cycles (2013–14, 2015–16): diabetes 0.946/0.938/0.924, CKD 0.959/0.968/0.962, anaemia 0.972/0.994/0.983, iron deficiency 0.989/–/0.972, hyperuricaemia 0.943/0.958/0.955, hypercholesterolaemia 0.905/0.883/0.886 and ALT elevation 0.872/0.880/0.877 (Fig. 5; Table 3). A correction: the previous version quoted 0.721 for ALT in 2017–18; that value came from registry 14.0.0, not the frozen v7. These laboratory-defined labels overlap the inputs and test criterion recognition; against self-reported physician diagnosis, AUROC was 0.90–0.92 for diabetes and 0.75–0.82 for kidney disease. Examination weights changed laboratory-defined AUROCs by ≤0.015 but lowered self-reported kidney disease in 2017–18 from 0.816 to 0.744 (Supplementary Table S7). Among guideline-healthy adults (30-analyte filter, Supplementary Note 5), the engine asserted a disease in 1/160, 1/246 and 0/79 (2/485, 0.4%); because the filter covers 30 analytes, this is a lower bound on false assertions. Separately, 0/1,000 synthetic guideline-normal panels produced a disease (a regression check, not evidence).

##### 3.6 External cohorts and case reports

On the UCI thyroid referral set AUROC was 0.979 (0.974–0.983) for hypothyroidism and 0.798 (0.783–0.814) for hyperthyroidism; on the HCV cohort 0.925 (0.881–0.962) for any liver disease; on ILPD 0.704 (0.661–0.747); in the MIMIC-IV demo top-5/10 recall was 47.5/62.1% (v7 re-run); on 50 PMC case reports the specific diagnosis was in the top five in 26% and the correct syndrome in 68% (Table 3; full results in Supplementary Note 10).

##### 3.7 Pre-specified negative result: sepsis

In PhysioNet/CinC 2019, the engine's sepsis score from first-24-h laboratory values discriminated Sepsis-3 onset with AUROC 0.600 (0.575–0.625) in set A, 0.610 (0.583–0.640) in quarantine set B and 0.588 (0.559–0.616) in set B2, versus 0.691, 0.712 and 0.679 for a supervised logistic model on the same features (Fig. 6). Guideline-sourced SIRS/qSOFA rules added afterwards (Amendment 15; developed on A, validated once on B2) added about 0.04 AUROC and are labelled post hoc. Hemoglyph is not a sepsis predictor.

##### 3.8 Blinded comparison with an unnamed frontier LLM

On 400 stays (291 with scorable labels; 596 labels) an unnamed frontier LLM, accessed through an agent platform that does not disclose the model, received the identical panel, age and sex and returned ten ranked diagnoses, blinded to labels (Supplementary Note 2). Recall@5—the pre-specified primary comparison—was 60.7% (56.5–65.0) for Hemoglyph versus 59.6% (55.3–63.6), difference +1.2 points (−2.5 to +5.1; cluster-adjusted McNemar p = 0.54). The LLM was better at rank 1 (28.2% vs 24.0%, p = 0.013) and rank 3 (50.8% vs 47.0%, p = 0.041); the engine at rank 10 (72.0% vs 65.1%, p = 0.0002) and left fewer labels absent (21.8% vs 34.9%); mean reciprocal rank 0.391 vs 0.408 (Fig. 7; Table 5). Crediting either engine tier—a post-hoc metric with no LLM counterpart—gave 69.8% vs 59.6%. An earlier comparison with engine v5.1 had favoured Hemoglyph (64.4%, p = 0.009); the v7 correctness changes removed that advantage. The engine led for GI bleeding (84 vs 50%), diabetes (50 vs 37%) and hypokalaemia (59 vs 41%); the LLM for metabolic acidosis (79 vs 50%), hyperkalaemia (90 vs 70%) and DKA (83 vs 73%). On 40 re-read panels the LLM's agreement with itself was 85.7% (κ = 0.69) and its recall@5 varied by ±5 points across reads and prompts—more than the engine–LLM difference. Both flagged 39/39 CAP critical values in 50 stays, a shared safety floor.

##### 3.9 Calibration, subgroups and robustness

Raw scores were uncalibrated (pooled AUROC 0.797; reliability and decision curves before and after cross-fitted isotonic regression in Fig. 8); subgroup recall by age, sex and unit type, unit-invariance and perturbation results are given in Supplementary Note 11 and Table S4.

##### 3.10 Verification: executable invariants and source concordance

Two further checks were added after the freeze; neither alters a frozen number. First, a property-based test suite
(`tests/test_properties.py`, engine v8.1) states invariants that any correct laboratory-interpretation engine must satisfy
and checks them on randomised panels rather than hand-written vignettes: determinism (identical output on repeated runs),
unit invariance (SI and conventional units give the same ranking, tolerance 10⁻³), directional monotonicity (a more extreme
value never lowers the probability of the finding it defines; 14 analyte–direction pairs including creatinine, glucose up and
down, potassium up and down, sodium up and down, haemoglobin, ALT, bicarbonate, leukocytes up and down, platelets),
absent-analyte invariance (a syndrome is never asserted from an analyte that is not present) and context invariants
(documented CKD never raises acute kidney injury; borderline-value flags behave as specified). All 21 tests pass. The suite
exposed one genuine defect that the 353 adversarial vignettes had not: a well-supported renal finding was capped beneath a
presumptive explainer scoring below 0.80, so the leading renal probability fell as creatinine rose from 1.6 to 2.2 mg/dL; the
fix applies an existing rule uniformly and passed the full gate (eICU 48.4/61.0/74.1% versus 47.9/60.8/74.1%, inside the v7
confidence intervals). These tests establish logical consistency, not clinical correctness: a mistranscribed threshold passes
every invariant.

Second, a source-concordance audit asked whether the rule transcribes what its cited source says (Methods; Supplementary
Note 8). Of 200 rules sampled under a frozen protocol (100 curated anchors, 100 candidates), only 16 could be resolved from
the locators the registry provides: 15 were discordant (7 attribution errors among curated anchors, such as a hypernatraemia
rule citing the hyponatraemia guideline, and 8 thresholds or definitions absent from an on-topic source, including a
candidate AKI "criterion" defined by a fixed creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL and attributed to KDIGO 2012, which defines AKI only by
change from baseline) and 1 concordant. The remaining 184 (92%, 95% CI 87.4–95.1) were unresolvable—12 with no retrievable
text, 55 with only a title or abstract, 46 citing only generic methodological standards (WHO essential diagnostics list, CLSI
EP28/C59) that cannot contain a disease-specific threshold, and 71 landing pages. Because an attribution error is visible from
a title while concordance needs the full text, the 15/16 ratio is not an estimate of the registry's discordance rate; the
defensible statements are that at least 7.5% (4.6–12.0) of sampled rules are demonstrably discordant and that the registry,
as released, is not auditable rule-by-rule from its own locators. The audit was performed by a single declared rater (an AI agent under the first author's direction); the protocol's second-rater arm did not yield a valid dataset and is not reported. No inter-rater reliability is therefore claimed for the audit, and its two figures (92% unverifiable, ≥ 7.5% discordant) should be read as one rater's finding under a frozen protocol that anyone can re-run.

#### R5 Discussion

A guideline-transcribed rule engine with no training data recovered 61% of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses in five suggestions from laboratory values alone (78% on the post-hoc panel-supported subset), reproduced screening AUROCs across three NHANES cycles while asserting disease in at most one adult per healthy cohort, and was statistically indistinguishable from an unnamed frontier LLM on the pre-specified metric, with the LLM stronger at rank 1 and the engine at coverage. Its advantages are determinism, local execution and traceability to a cited rule, not accuracy. It failed, as pre-specified, to predict sepsis.

Five findings shape how these numbers should be read. First, the ~60% ceiling is largely a property of the reference standard: a quarter of admission-level labels were not inferable from the first panel, and a supervised model trained on the labels themselves did not exceed 0.82 AUROC for outcome-defined groups. The panel-supported metric was defined after the ceiling was observed and is structurally optimistic; we present it alongside, not instead of, the flat metric. Second, the untrained engine matched supervised models wherever a guideline definition exists and lost wherever the label is an outcome (sepsis, bleeding, withdrawal) learned from hidden correlates—the expected division of labour between knowledge and learning, which argues for hybrid rather than replacement designs. Third, the LLM and the engine make different errors: differences are small where a single analyte defines the label (renal impairment 83% vs 81%) and large where a multi-analyte pattern must be assembled or where the LLM prefers a broader term; the LLM's test–retest variability exceeded the between-system difference. Fourth, the ablation locates the result in the rule-engine vote and the curated syndrome constellations, written by a non-clinician first author from guideline criteria and not independently validated; the 33,000-rule registry adds one to three points. We therefore describe Hemoglyph as guideline-*transcribed* rather than guideline-derived: the result is what such a transcription achieves, not what the guidelines themselves would achieve. The expert floor (fused score ≥0.90 × rule vote) means the learned layers can modulate but not veto a rule—a deliberate bias towards explicit criteria that trades the possibility of a learned layer correcting a wrong rule for the guarantee that every surfaced diagnosis is traceable to one. Fifth, a prevalence-ranking baseline reaches recall@5 of 51% and overtakes the engine at k = 10; the engine's measurable contribution is ordering (NDCG@5 56.5% vs 39.0%; recall@3 47.9% vs 36.1%) and per-stay specificity (precision@5 14.2% vs 10.8%), and precision against an incomplete problem list remains low in absolute terms.

Our design addresses criticisms levelled at recent LLM benchmarks—unstable metrics, leakage and post-hoc selection [26,31]. Releases were frozen with manifests; quarantine data were run once; a failed round is reported with its original criteria and the subsequent weakening to non-inferiority is disclosed; the post-hoc sepsis additions are labelled. The cost of correctness is visible: moving from the product release to v7 cost three points of flat recall, almost entirely because a single creatinine no longer asserts CKD and a normal leukocyte count no longer yields a syndrome; our first submission reported superiority over the LLM against an earlier engine version, and the apparent advantage was not retained when the frozen v7 engine was evaluated. Compared with Isabel and DXplain, which take symptoms and have been evaluated on 36–201 cases [13–16,32], this study isolates laboratory-only reasoning on more than 20,000 real encounters and releases its knowledge base under an open licence at a time when the main public executable-guideline repository has been withdrawn. Because the rules were AI-drafted, open release is a precondition for—not a substitute for—expert audit; every leaf carries a source locator, so a clinician can accept, amend or reject a rule and the change is versioned.

Where might such an engine be used? Not as an autonomous diagnostician. Its natural positions are the post-analytical step of a laboratory information system—interpretive comments and critical-pattern alerts generated locally—and a reproducible, auditable baseline against which LLM-based interpretation can be measured. The two architectures could be combined: a language model to extract and normalise free-text results (the step at which our automatic parser lost half the PMC cases) and a rule engine to reason over them. We chose laboratory-only inputs as the primary arm because documented history inflates recall mainly by echoing the diagnosis already written down (+23 points for labels named in the history versus +1 otherwise). We did not tune the engine to the sepsis task after the pre-specified result because an outcome-fitted rule would have been a supervised model in disguise.

Post-freeze proposals were tested against the frozen artefacts before any code changed (Section 2.7; Supplementary Notes 7 and 9); a separate supervised sepsis companion reached held-out AUROC 0.654/0.631 versus the engine's 0.610/0.588. The verification results reframe what this paper can claim. Executable invariants are a genuine methodological advantage of a rule engine over an LLM—they can be stated, run on every release and violated only visibly—and they found a defect that vignettes had missed. The source-concordance audit, however, shows the limit of such verification: the registry's locators are organisation-level URLs and generic standards, so most rules cannot be checked against their source at all, and where they could be checked the error was usually one of attribution. Concordance is a necessary, not sufficient, condition for validity—a rule can quote its source exactly and still encode an outdated threshold—so an independent clinical audit remains the essential next step, and section-level locators are a precondition for it. Consequent registry actions (planned for release 15, none altering the frozen v7 results): section- or table-level locators for every source, re-sourcing or retirement of the 46 sampled candidates—and the class they represent—whose only citation is a generic methodological standard, and correction of the five curated-anchor attribution errors identified. The output design (a rule identifier and source citation for every entry) is consistent with the transparency principle of Criterion 4 of the FDA's January 2026 clinical-decision-support guidance; determining regulatory status is outside the scope of this paper and no claim about it is made. None of these post-freeze versions (v8, v8.1) has been through a quarantine round, and no frozen number here derives from them.

What would change the evidence level? A rule audit by independent clinicians on a random sample with inter-rater agreement; the credentialed full eICU and MIMIC-IV releases, on which the released scripts run unchanged; and a clinician-in-the-loop crossover study measuring accuracy, time and automation bias, for which a protocol has been drafted. Until then, the claims of this paper are bounded to retrospective, laboratory-only performance.

##### 4.1 Limitations

The knowledge base has not been audited rule-by-rule by clinicians, and the non-clinical source-concordance audit reported here shows that 92% of sampled rules cannot even be checked against their cited source from the locator provided, while at least 7.5% are demonstrably discordant; the components that carry the result—272 anchor rules and ~900 syndrome constellations—were written by a non-clinician first author; 34 sources lack a locator; the Bayesian priors are hand-coded without a per-value citation table. No version-control history exists for the development period, so the claim that no change was outcome-fitted rests on the dated change log. Only the open demo subsets of eICU and MIMIC-IV were used; the reference standards are admission-level problem lists and ICD codes, not adjudicated panel-level diagnoses. The LLM comparison is an author-run self-baseline through an agent platform that does not disclose the model, so it is not reproducible against a named model; it used two prompts, no retrieval augmentation and no clinician adjudication, and the model's training data may include the public eICU demo. The quarantine criteria were weakened after round 1 failed; the panel-supported metric is post hoc; the OCR front end was not evaluated; laboratory-defined NHANES labels overlap the inputs; raw scores are uncalibrated; the external cohorts are small and ILPD discrimination was modest. No clinician-in-the-loop evaluation was performed. Iranian populations and non-English laboratory conventions were not evaluated. The first author is a secondary-school student with a financial interest in the commercial product, and the system, including 99.2% of the rules, was developed with AI-agent assistance. Hemoglyph is a research prototype with no regulatory clearance.

#### R6 §3.5 Component ablation

Two components carry the signal (Table 1; Supplementary Table S12): removing the rule-engine vote cost 12.1 points of flat recall@5 (−14.4 to −9.8) and removing the syndrome layer 9.5 points (−11.1 to −7.9). The Bayesian network contributed 1.3 points; the other layers and any fusion-weight profile changed recall by ≤ 1.4 points. Restricting the registry to the 272 curated anchors (32,911 AI-drafted rules off) lowered recall@5 by 0.9 points (−2.1 to +0.2) with the syndrome layer on and 3.1 points with it off. The headline therefore rests on the hand-curated components, and registry scale is not claimed as a merit.

#### R6 §3.6 Community survey, external cohorts and the pre-specified negative result

In 3,000 NHANES adults per cycle, unweighted AUROCs reproduced across the development cycle (2017–18) and the two quarantine cycles (2013–14, 2015–16): diabetes 0.946/0.938/0.924, CKD 0.959/0.968/0.962, anaemia 0.972/0.994/0.983, hyperuricaemia 0.943/0.958/0.955, hypercholesterolaemia 0.905/0.883/0.886, ALT elevation 0.872/0.880/0.877 (Fig. 5; Table 4). These laboratory-defined labels overlap the inputs; against self-reported physician diagnosis AUROC was 0.90–0.92 for diabetes and 0.75–0.82 for kidney disease. Among guideline-healthy adults the engine asserted a disease in 2/485 (0.4%). External cohorts (UCI thyroid 0.979/0.798, HCV 0.925, ILPD 0.704, MIMIC-IV demo top-5 47.5%, PMC case reports 26% specific/68% syndrome) are in Table 4 and Supplementary Note 10. In PhysioNet/CinC 2019 the engine's sepsis score discriminated Sepsis-3 onset with AUROC 0.600, 0.610 and 0.588 in sets A, B and B2 versus 0.691, 0.712 and 0.679 for a supervised logistic model on the same features (Fig. 6); Hemoglyph is not a sepsis predictor and was not tuned to the task afterwards. Raw scores were uncalibrated (pooled AUROC 0.797; Fig. 8); subgroup and robustness results are in Supplementary Note 11.
