## Abstract

Clinical decision support systems (CDSS) for laboratory medicine must reason under conflicting and incomplete evidence, remain explainable and, increasingly, operate without transmitting patient data. We present Hemoglyph, a research-prototype desktop CDSS whose deterministic inference engine runs fully offline and that interprets structured laboratory panels with a five-layer reasoning engine (deterministic guideline rules, a clustered Bayesian network, adaptive fuzzy logic, a clinical knowledge graph and 100 published risk models), fused by fixed weights and a constrained Theory-of-Evidence layer that uses binary frames, Yager's combination rule (conflict is assigned to ignorance), explicit abstention and a confidence-shrunk rank score. The knowledge base holds 2,045 analytes, 6,750 clinical entities and 33,183 executable rules from 785 cited sources; **99.2% of the rules were drafted with AI assistance and await independent specialist validation**, 0.8% are manually curated guideline anchors; component ablation shows that the headline results rest on the curated anchors and syndrome layer, not on the AI-drafted bulk (switching the latter off changed top-5 recall by 0.9 points with the syndrome layer present and 3.1 points without it). All results concern structured analyte–value pairs; the OCR front end was not evaluated. Under an internally pre-specified analysis plan (time-stamped and hash-frozen, not publicly registered) with cryptographic freezes and two one-shot quarantine rounds (the first failed its superiority criteria, which were then changed to non-inferiority, so the second round is supportive rather than confirmatory), the frozen engine was evaluated on 2,314 ICU stays (eICU-CRD demo), 9,000 adults in three NHANES cycles (survey-weighted), 14,420 PhysioNet/CinC 2019 sepsis patients and external thyroid, hepatitis C, liver and case-report cohorts. From laboratory values alone, 60.8% of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses appeared in the top-5 differential (74.1% top-10); a post-hoc secondary metric restricted to diagnoses that the first panel could support reached 78.0% / 91.8%. Screening AUROCs were 0.88–0.99 for six laboratory-defined targets and 0.87–0.88 for ALT elevation, and reproduced across cycles; among 485 guideline-healthy adults the engine asserted disease twice (2/485). Label-group precision@5 against the ICU problem list was 14.2% (F1 23.1%, NDCG@5 56.5%; a prevalence-ranking baseline reached recall@5 51.3% and NDCG@5 39.0% and overtook the engine at k = 10, so the engine's contribution is ordering, not coverage). On 50 published case reports the end-to-end parser placed the final diagnosis in the top-5 in 10% of cases (44% with the syndrome layer only), showing that free-text extraction, not reasoning, is the bottleneck. In a blinded, paired comparison on 400 stays with identical inputs and stay-clustered inference, the frozen engine and an unnamed frontier LLM (accessed through an agent platform that does not disclose the model) were equivalent on the primary metric (recall@5 60.7% vs 59.6%, p = 0.54); the LLM ranked more diagnoses first, omitted more labels (34.9% vs 21.8%) and was only moderately self-consistent on re-reading (κ 0.69); the engine is deterministic. The engine did not predict Sepsis-3 onset (AUROC 0.59–0.64; SIRS/qSOFA rules added after this negative result were validated once), the components that carry the result (272 hand-curated anchors and the syndrome layer) have not been independently audited, Iranian populations were not evaluated, raw scores are over-confident ranks rather than probabilities, and no prospective or clinician-in-the-loop evaluation has been performed. Code, rules and evaluation scripts are released under open licences.

Keywords: clinical decision support system; laboratory medicine; Theory of Evidence; Yager combination rule; abstention; knowledge-based reasoning; pre-specified analysis plan; ablation; large language models; explainability.

## 1. Introduction

With the expansion of artificial intelligence in medicine, clinical decision support systems have become one of its most prominent applications. These systems serve as advisors to physicians and researchers, facilitating more accurate and safer decisions in the identification of disease. By integrating clinical and laboratory data they are used for diagnosis, screening, treatment adjustment, prescribing, monitoring, alerting on critical values and drug interactions, and reminding clinicians of guideline recommendations; they also reduce medical error (Sutton et al., 2020).

Clinical decision support was introduced by Ledley and Lusted (1959), who applied symbolic logic and probability to the diagnosis of heart disease. Dambro (1972) developed a CDSS for acute abdominal pain that marked the first application of the simple Bayesian rule to generate diagnoses, and in 1974 the CASNET system employed if–then rules to guide antibiotic selection. These systems relied on explicit knowledge bases and rule-based reasoning but could not distinguish absence of evidence from conflicting evidence and behaved unpredictably under uncertainty. To address the requirement of earlier methods for a single, definitive probability, Dempster (1967) introduced upper and lower probabilities, in which each hypothesis is represented by an interval whose width expresses ignorance; Shafer (1976) formalised this into the Dempster–Shafer theory of evidence (DST). Because Dempster's rule normalises conflict away and may place excessive confidence in a single hypothesis, Yager (1987) proposed assigning the conflicting mass to ignorance so that no decision is forced when the evidence disagrees.

Recent work has revisited hybrid knowledge-based designs. Maqsood et al. (2026) fused a physician-extracted rule base confirming 59 conditions from laboratory results with an XGBoost likely-diagnosis model and SHAP explanations, trained on 593,055 primary-care patients; Nambison et al. (2024) built a fuzzy-logic system interpreting pathology reports for 17 diseases; Damkliang (2025) released an AI-based screening framework for iron-deficiency anaemia and thalassaemia; and machine-learning models on complete-blood-count 'fingerprints' have been proposed for opportunistic anaemia and leukaemia screening (Scientific Reports, 2025). Hybrid rule-plus-learning architectures are explicitly recommended by recent scoping reviews of rule-based CDSS (PMC12482788, 2025). In parallel, LLMs have been shown to interpret laboratory reports fluently but not reproducibly, to misclassify values as high or low even when reference ranges are supplied, and to fabricate references (Cadamuro et al., 2023; Kurstjens et al., 2024; Hager et al., 2024). Mature diagnostic generators such as Isabel and DXplain have recently been compared with unnamed frontier models and found within a few points on recall while being reproducible (Bridges, 2024, 2025; MGB, 2025; Bond et al., 2012); none of these studies isolated laboratory-only reasoning, and none was pre-specified. Meanwhile the public infrastructure for executable guideline knowledge has contracted: the AHRQ CDS Connect repository and authoring tool were taken offline on 28 April 2025 (continuing only as a community edition under HL7), the ECRI Guidelines Trust has been unavailable since 2024, and in a search of these repositories, the WHO SMART guideline digital adaptation kits and NICE's licensed content (October 2026) we found no openly licensed, executable rule set for laboratory-panel interpretation.

A review of the literature therefore indicates that although individual studies address specific aspects of laboratory-report interpretation—OCR, single-disease classification, evidence combination, or LLM explanation—none provides a comprehensive, integrated and independently evaluated solution. In this paper we present Hemoglyph, a framework that combines multiple OCR back-ends and a consistency solver with a five-layer cross-reasoning engine and a constrained Theory-of-Evidence fusion, and—unlike our earlier description of the system—we report a pre-specified, multi-dataset evaluation of the frozen engine, including a quarantine round that failed, a pre-specified negative result, a component ablation and a blinded comparison with a frontier LLM on identical inputs. The scope of the evaluation is **structured laboratory values**; the optical-character-recognition front end of the product is described but was not evaluated. The contributions are: (i) an architecture in which every output is traceable to a cited rule and in which conflict is handled by Yager-type combination with explicit abstention; (ii) a quantified account of the ceiling imposed by the reference standard in real ICU data; (iii) evidence that an untrained knowledge-based engine matches supervised models wherever a guideline definition exists and is equivalent to a unnamed frontier LLM on the pre-specified laboratory-only metric while being deterministic; and (iv) full disclosure of the provenance of an AI-assisted knowledge base and of the limits of the evidence.

All reported results concern the inference engine fed with structured analyte–value pairs; the OCR front end of the desktop product was not evaluated, and the free-text parser tested on case reports performed poorly (Section 4.5), so none of the figures should be read as end-to-end performance from a scanned report. The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the Hemoglyph system. Section 3 describes the evaluation methodology, including pre-specification, freezes and quarantine. Section 4 reports results. Section 5 discusses principal findings, comparison with prior work, clinical implications, the changes made since the earlier version of the system, and limitations.

## 2. The Hemoglyph System

Hemoglyph is a research-prototype CDSS. In the product it ingests images of blood-test reports through multiple OCR back-ends and extracts the laboratory parameters; the engine evaluated here receives structured values directly. It then performs an integrated analysis by simultaneously applying 33,183 executable rules drawn from 785 cited guideline sources, together with statistical, fuzzy and graph-based reasoning and 100 published risk scores, to rank candidate diagnoses and provide evidence-based, explainable outputs. Fig. 1 shows the core components: (1) a modular native architecture (Python 3.11, PySide6/QML, compiled with Nuitka); (2) a structured knowledge base (2,045 analytes with 85,828 aliases, 6,750 clinical entities, 33,183 executable rules); (3) a hybrid OCR layer producing JSON output validated by a consistency solver with 27 physiological constraints (described, not evaluated); (4) a five-layer cross-inference engine with fixed weighted fusion; (5) a constrained Theory-of-Evidence layer (binary frames, Yager's rule, abstention, calibrated scoring); and (6) an expert syndrome layer with causal attribution and guideline-semantics guards (engine v7).

Fig. 1. Core components of the Hemoglyph system.

### 2.1 Architecture and modules

The application is composed of independent modules: image extraction and pre-processing (`ocr.py`, `_ocr_support.py`); a consistency solver (`ocr_consistency.py`); an extraction-confidence model (`ocr_confidence.py`); the guideline-analysis and inference engine (`analyse_gidlines.py`), which hosts the rule engine, Bayesian network, fuzzy engine, knowledge graph, fusion, ToE and expert syndrome layers; a 100-model risk predictor (`prdicter.py`); unit conversion (`calcu.py`); and licence management and registry integrity (`license_engine.py`, `cache_registry.py`). The system runs entirely on the local CPU in about one second per panel (median 380–480 ms and p95 ≈ 0.9 s per panel on a single x86-64 core in the 2-GB-RAM evaluation sandbox, with no GPU; the sandbox processed one eICU stay per second including I/O. The desktop requirement is any 64-bit Windows machine with 4 GB RAM; no dedicated hardware). The inference engine, the registries and the licence module make no network calls (licence validation is node-locked and offline); the optional multimodal OCR back-end is a remote service: when the user enables it, the report image leaves the device, so the release makes no offline claim for that path and "fully offline" applies to the structured-input engine evaluated here. The desktop application is distributed under a proprietary licence with node-locked activation; the engine source, registries and evaluation scripts are the open components. The optional local chat assistant is disabled in the evaluated release (1.3.1); all inference reported here is produced by the deterministic engine.

### 2.2 Structured knowledge base

The knowledge base performs inference independently of any language model, relying exclusively on structured data and deterministic rules. Two Brotli-compressed JSON registries hold (a) a feature registry of 2,045 analytes with LOINC codes, UCUM canonical units, English and Persian synonyms (85,828 aliases) and age-, sex- and pregnancy-specific reference intervals; and (b) a disease–guideline registry (version 14.1.0) of 6,750 clinical entities—diseases, laboratory findings and biomarker patterns—linked to 34,673 rule records and 785 cited sources (society guidelines such as KDIGO, ADA, ATA, AASLD/EASL, ACC/AHA, ESC, ACR and CLSI, and standard texts). Rules are declarative (analyte, operator, threshold, unit, population, evidence role and weight, source) and are organised in four layers: screening (10,979), diagnostic criterion (6,140), exclusion criterion (6,220) and staging/severity (9,844). A code-level curation layer rejects malformed rules at load time.

The registries were drafted with the assistance of a large language model and curated by the first author through automated audits; they have not been reviewed by an independent expert panel (see Limitations). Concretely, 'AI assistance' means: for each source the first author prompted a general-purpose LLM with the guideline text or citation and a fixed JSON schema; the returned candidate records (analyte, operator, threshold, unit, population gate, evidence role, disease identifier, source locator) were not individually edited by a human but accepted or rejected by automated audits (schema validity, analyte present in the feature registry, unit convertible, threshold within physiological range, no duplicate or contradiction, context gate where the guideline requires one, source locator resolvable) and carry the status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`; the 272 anchors were written or line-edited by hand from the primary guideline text; demotions and retirements followed rule-level defect reports, never outcome data. No record exists of what proportion of candidate rules a human read, so the bulk is described as AI-drafted and automatically audited, not human-reviewed. An 'executable' rule is a record with a machine-evaluable condition on at least one analyte, a disease identifier and the `executable: true` flag; retired, template and empty-expression records are kept for provenance and never evaluated. Fig. 2 shows the curation flow. Of 34,673 rule records in release 14.1.0, 1,487 are retired (1,139 at 13.3.0, 171 domain artefacts and 164 template artefacts at 13.4.0, 9 non-specific single-marker rules, 3 empty expressions, 1 at 14.0) and 3 candidates lack an executable expression, leaving 33,183 executable rules (Supplementary Table S10 reconciles every count against the live file). Of these, 272 (0.8%) are manually curated guideline anchors, 13 are former anchors demoted to candidate status at 13.4.2/14.0, and 32,898 (99.1%) carry the status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`, which the engine exposes in every output so that the origin of knowledge is transparent; 55 executable rules carry no evidence-tier field. Earlier drafts quoted 1,474 retired and 32,901 candidates (the latter counted the 3 non-executable candidates; the former omitted the 13 demotions); the figures here are computed from the shipped file. The source table lists 785 sources (781 at 13.4.1; four added at 14.0–14.1); 388 of them are cited by at least one executable rule, the remainder only by retired rules or disease records. A provenance audit of the 781 sources in release 13.4.1 resolved 112 DOIs via Crossref, reached 533 landing pages, found 63 URL-only and 34 locator-less sources, and 39 that were unreachable from our environment (publisher sites that block automated access; unreachable is not fabricated); nine invalid DOIs were removed and 28 rules were flagged `unverified_source`. In the desktop interface every differential entry shows its evidence tier and status (anchor / candidate / unverified source) next to the score, and the validation status of each rule is machine-readable in the registry (`status`, `evidence_tier`, `executable`).

### 2.3 Extraction layer (OCR) and consistency solver

The extraction layer uses a hybrid method to reduce reading error. In the local pipeline, images are pre-processed before being passed to Tesseract, and an error analyser based on fuzzy matching and the alias registry corrects the extracted values. In the structured pipeline, a multimodal model produces JSON output (name, value, unit, reference range) and downstream inference is strictly limited to this structured data. To prevent "flawless inference on wrong numbers", a consistency solver defines 27 physiological and mathematical constraints—haematological and biochemical identities (e.g. MCH = Hb/RBC; anion gap; calculated LDL) and monotonicity requirements—and models common OCR errors (misplaced decimal, dropped digit) with explicit edit costs. A minimum-joint-cost search identifies a correction satisfying all constraints and uses culprit/exoneration reasoning to localise the erroneous field. **The OCR layer and the consistency solver were not part of the quantitative evaluation reported here**; all datasets were ingested as structured values, and we make no claim about OCR accuracy, about the error-detection rate of the solver, or about offline operation of the multimodal pipeline. Every claim in Sections 4–5 therefore applies to structured laboratory values only.

### 2.4 Five-layer cross-inference engine and weighted fusion

Five layers score every candidate entity independently: (1) the rule engine, a population-aware deterministic evaluation of the registry across the four rule layers from screening to staging; (2) a clustered Bayesian network (75 disease nodes, 30 evidence clusters) whose prevalence priors (constants or age bands, e.g. type 2 diabetes 0.08, CKD 0.10) and likelihood ratios (68 nodes; e.g. lipase ≥ 3 × ULN, LR+ 18 for acute pancreatitis) are hand-coded from diagnostic-accuracy literature cited in code comments—none is estimated from patient data; (3) an adaptive fuzzy layer (35 rules, 20 clinical patterns) that grades the distance of each value from its reference limits and abstains when no rule covers the analyte; (4) a clinical knowledge graph (97 nodes, 112 cause–effect edges) that propagates support between pathophysiologically linked entities; and (5) 100 published risk models (FIB-4, APRI, eGFR CKD-EPI 2021, corrected calcium, albumin-corrected anion gap, TyG, HOMA-IR and others). Layer outputs are fused by a fixed weighted ensemble set by the first author as a judgement that explicit criteria should dominate (no weight was derived from or tuned on any evaluation dataset; sensitivity in Table 5)—0.35 rule engine, 0.25 Bayesian, 0.20 fuzzy, 0.10 graph, 0.10 risk models, with domain-specific profiles for toxicology, critical care, autoimmune, oncology, endocrine and cardiometabolic entities—followed by discord-aware shrinkage: when layers disagree the fused score is pulled towards the sceptical cluster by up to 45%, and a score supported by a single layer is discounted by 30%. No weight was fitted to outcome data; the weights were fixed before any real-data evaluation and a pre-specified weight sweep (Phase 7) produced no stable improvement and was abandoned.

### 2.5 Constrained Theory-of-Evidence layer

Because of the uncertainty inherent in clinical data, the collective voting layer is not simple voting or standard DST but a constrained variant with four structural modifications. First, to avoid the exponential cost of a single frame containing every diagnosis, a binary frame—presence, absence, ignorance—is defined for each disease. Second, Yager's rule replaces Dempster's rule: the conflict mass K is assigned to ignorance (Θ) rather than renormalised among hypotheses, so that when the five evidence sources disagree the system withholds a definitive decision instead of amplifying one side. Third, abstention is an explicit state: layers that abstain are excluded from both the conflict computation and the combination, and their weights are redistributed among the layers that spoke. Fourth, the final ranking is determined by the fused point score of Section 2.4 (a rank score with a confidence shrinkage step; it is not a calibrated probability, see Section 4.7), while belief and plausibility are used only to report the evidence status of each candidate (confirmed, probable, insufficient). Reliability discounting is applied to each source before combination. This design reduces the risk of confidently wrong outputs — it cannot eliminate it, because a wrong rule firing alone is still a wrong rule — and is one reason the engine asserted disease in 0 of 1,000 synthetic healthy panels and in at most 1 of 246 guideline-healthy NHANES adults (Section 4). Section 4.8 quantifies the contribution of each component by ablation.

### 2.6 Expert syndrome layer, causal attribution and ranking hygiene (engine v6–v7)

The fusion also enforces an expert floor: the fused score of a diagnosis cannot fall below 0.90 × the rule-layer vote, so the four other layers can modulate but never veto a guideline rule. This is a deliberate bias towards explicit criteria—it guarantees that every surfaced diagnosis is traceable to a rule, at the price that a learned layer can never correct a wrong rule—and it is one reason the ablation (Section 4.8) finds those layers nearly inert. Above the fused score sits an expert syndrome layer of approximately 900 hand-tuned multi-analyte constellations, each with a tiered anchor strength (anchor 0.95, strong 0.88, pattern 0.80, supportive 0.72, screen 0.45), operators (absent, between, negated, present) and context predicates (pregnancy, age, sex, medication, condition, family history, encounter). This layer can strengthen, weaken or guard a candidate but cannot introduce a diagnosis that the rule layer never proposed. Engine versions v6 and v7 added a ranking-hygiene stage that enforces guideline semantics: a disease–finding explanation graph lets a covering diagnosis explain its downstream findings (diabetic ketoacidosis explains hyperglycaemia, acidosis and the anion gap, which are then shown as `explained_by` rather than as competitors); an anchor finding such as a raised INR never loses its critical status; a diagnosis with insufficient evidence cannot bury a finding; a single creatinine yields "reduced GFR, chronicity undetermined" per KDIGO rather than chronic kidney disease; and a single troponin yields "myocardial injury, acuity undetermined" per the fourth universal definition of myocardial infarction. No syndrome may be asserted from a normal analyte. The output is a two-tier differential—specific diagnoses and umbrella syndromes—each with explained findings, an evidence state, cited sources and suggested next tests (maximum 25 entries). The engine is deterministic: byte-identical output was obtained in 50 of 50 repeated stays and across freezes.

### 2.8 Versioning and releases

Every release is versioned jointly for engine and registry and must pass a regression gate before any real-data run: 689 unit tests, three adversarial vignette batteries (151, 124 and 78 cases), a 1,000-panel healthy sweep, a 755-pair perturbation battery and an SI/conventional unit-twin battery. The pass criterion is non-regression: no vignette, perturbation pair or healthy panel that passed in the previous release may fail, and the healthy sweep must stay at zero assertions. The two residual failures are longstanding and documented: in the third vignette battery the engine ranks alcoholic pancreatitis second behind alcohol-use disorder (77/78), and in the perturbation battery thiazide-associated hypovolaemic hyponatraemia is brittle in 2 of 5 noisy replicates (753/755; 752/755 in release 14.1; all failures are replicates of the same thiazide-hyponatraemia vignette, Supplementary Note 4). The evaluated release is engine v7 with registry 14.1.0; the shipped product release 1.3.1 uses registry 13.4.2. Engine changes C1–C16 between 13.4.2 and v7 are listed with dates in Supplementary Table S1; none was fitted to outcomes and no rule was written for a specific missed case (the one persistent failure in the adversarial battery, alcoholic pancreatitis, was deliberately left unpatched).

## 3. Evaluation Methodology

### 3.1 Pre-specification, freezes and quarantine

All analyses follow an internal statistical analysis plan (SAP) that fixed metrics, label maps, thresholds and comparators; fifteen dated amendments record every subsequent change with justification (Supplementary Note 1). The SAP was time-stamped and hash-frozen in the project repository but not deposited in a public registry, so we write 'pre-specified' rather than 'pre-specified'. Before each quarantine round the code and registries were frozen with a SHA-256 manifest (261 and 316 files). Quarantine datasets had never been run before the freeze and were run exactly once (Fig. 3a); the only exception is the MIMIC-IV demo, re-run post hoc with the frozen v7 for comparability and labelled as such. Round 1 (MIMIC-IV demo, NHANES 2013–14, PhysioNet sepsis set B) used superiority criteria over the previous release and failed two of them, because guideline-correctness changes lowered label recall for chronic kidney disease; the failure is reported with its original criteria. The criteria were then re-specified as non-inferiority with dated justification (Amendment 15, 2026-10-25, written after the Round-1 result was known but before any Round-2 file was opened; the Round-2 archive had been downloaded unread beforehand): NHANES AUROC within 0.03 of the previous release for each of six laboratory-defined targets (diabetes, CKD, anaemia, iron deficiency, hypercholesterolaemia, hyperuricaemia; ALT elevation was excluded a priori as definition-sensitive and its non-inferiority—0.877 vs 0.880—is reported post hoc) with ≤ 1% false assertions in healthy adults; sepsis AUROC not more than 0.02 below the previous release with a control flag rate ≤ 20%; and no new failure in the safety batteries, six further correctness changes were made (engine v7), the development gate showed parity, and Round 2 (NHANES 2015–16, sepsis set B2, healthy sweep with a new seed) met all criteria. Because the criteria were changed after Round 1 was observed, **Round 2 is labelled supportive rather than confirmatory**; Supplementary Table S2 lists every amendment with its date and whether it preceded or followed the observation of the data it concerns. Two data-loader defects discovered during Round 2 (patient identifiers stored as strings; a dropped derived NHANES variable) were corrected before scoring without touching the engine and are documented; the first NHANES run without the dropped variable is kept (`report_v7_run1_noACR.md`) and differs only in the CKD task, which could not be scored without it.

### 3.3 Metrics

In response to review we also report label-group precision@k, F1@k and NDCG@k (binary relevance, ideal DCG over the coded labels), the mean number of label-group assertions per stay (alert burden), per-group positive predictive value against the problem list, and a prevalence-ranking baseline that lists the groups in order of cohort frequency among those whose key analyte is present (Supplementary Table S11). Because the problem list is incomplete, precision and PPV are lower bounds. The primary metric in eICU is flat recall@k (k = 3, 5, 10) of clinician-listed diagnoses in the specific-diagnosis tier, computed over the 2,432 labels whose key analyte was present in the panel (of 2,707 coded labels; the 275 others are reported as 'analyte missing'). Secondary metrics are two-tier recall (either tier; introduced in Amendment 14), per-group AUROC of the group score, and **panel-supported recall** — recall restricted to labels whose first panel met a guideline criterion. Its thresholds were fixed in code before the script was run on any file, but the metric was conceived after the recall ceiling had been observed; it is therefore **post hoc and structurally optimistic** and is reported beside, never instead of, the flat metric. For screening datasets we report AUROC with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, sensitivity and specificity at 0.5, and the false-assertion rate among adults with all indices normal. For sepsis we report the AUROC of the sepsis-group score. Calibration is assessed by expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score before and after cross-fitted isotonic regression.

### 3.4 Comparators

Four comparators used identical inputs: (B1) a raw key-analyte threshold; (B2) gradient boosting and logistic regression trained on the same analytes with five-fold out-of-fold prediction; a registry-off ablation (syndrome layer only); and a general-purpose unnamed frontier LLM run by the authors through a chat interface (runs on 2026-10-21, -22 and -23 for n = 60/100/240; re-read 2026-10-24; alternative prompt 2026-10-25; no temperature or sampling parameters are exposed by the interface, so provider defaults apply; the LLM was accessed through the Arena.ai agent platform, which routes requests among several frontier model families (including GPT, Claude and Gemini) and does not expose the specific model or version to the user; 'unnamed frontier' therefore denotes a capability tier, not a verified identity, and the comparison cannot be reproduced against a named model version—a limitation stated in the Discussion), blinded to labels, with a fixed prompt requesting ten ranked diagnoses from the identical panel, age and sex, scored by the same label map and script; 40 panels were re-read one day later and read again with an alternative prompt to measure run-to-run and prompt-induced variation. The engine arm was originally scored with engine v5.1 and has been **re-scored with the frozen engine v7** without regenerating any LLM output. The full protocol, prompts, batching, blinding procedure, contamination analysis and all outputs are in Supplementary Note 2.

### 3.4b Post-freeze development version (v8)

Engine v8 (2026-10-26) differs from the frozen v7 in three presentation- or index-level additions—MELD/MELD-Na (Kamath 2001; Kim 2008; OPTN formula with sodium clamped to 125–137 mmol/L and creatinine capped at 4 mg/dL) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio as derived patterns with `supports = []`, and a `confidence_band`/`concise` field on each differential entry (bands ≥ 0.70 high, 0.60–0.69 moderate, 0.50–0.59 low, otherwise consider; descriptive, calibrated on the development set only)—and in registry 14.2.0, which re-sources 28 rules (9 source records) with Crossref-resolved DOIs and changes no rule logic, threshold, weight or executable flag (`eval/registry_patches/patch_14_2_0.py`). The sepsis companion (`eval/sepsis_ml/sepsis_risk_module.py`) uses the 5,000-patient public subsets of PhysioNet/CinC 2019; features are listed in the script; the logistic model is class-balanced L2 (C = 0.05); the gradient-boosting reference uses depth ≤ 4, ≤ 15 leaves, ≥ 300 samples per leaf, L2 = 5 and early stopping on 15% of set A. The alert threshold for the utility score was chosen on set A over a grid of score quantiles. Disclosure: the boosting hyper-parameters were tightened once after a first evaluation on set B showed over-fitting (training AUROC 0.997, set B 0.719); set B2 was not opened until the final configuration. Patient-level AUROC uses the maximum score over hours before the shifted label for septic patients and over all hours for non-septic patients; horizon AUROC compares the score exactly h hours before onset with a random hour of each non-septic patient. The v8 gate (`eval/results/gate_v8/`) repeats the v7 gate (three vignette batteries, 1,000-panel healthy sweep, 755-pair perturbation battery, unit tests, eICU labs-only run) with a pre-stated non-inferiority margin of 0.5 recall points.

### 3.5 Statistics

Labels are nested within stays (mean 1.05 scorable labels per stay in the full cohort; 2.05 in the LLM subset). All eICU proportions and paired differences are therefore reported with **stay-clustered inference**: cluster bootstrap over stays (4,000 resamples) for recall@k and for the engine–LLM difference, and the cluster-adjusted McNemar test of Durkalski et al. (2003) for the primary comparison; the intraclass correlation and design effect are reported. Per-group AUROCs are exploratory: Mann–Whitney p-values are adjusted by Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg procedures. NHANES estimates are given unweighted (as pre-specified) and **survey-weighted** with the MEC examination weight and PSU-within-stratum bootstrap intervals. Calibration is reported as calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large, Brier score with cluster-bootstrap CI, log-loss, ECE before and after cross-fitted isotonic regression, and decision curves. Sensitivity analyses vary the minimum panel size (8–25 analytes). Analyses used Python 3.11/3.13 with numpy, pandas, scipy and scikit-learn. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI (checklist in Supplement). Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI, adapted for a system that is not fitted to data (items on model training answered "not applicable" with justification), and the DECIDE-AI early-stage evaluation items were consulted for the Limitations; the completed checklist is Supplementary Note 5.7.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Regression gate and robustness

The frozen release passed 689/689 unit tests, 151/151, 124/124 and 77/78 adversarial vignettes, 753/755 perturbation pairs, the unit-twin battery and the healthy sweep (0 assertions in 1,000 panels). In 50 real stays the engine and the LLM both flagged 39/39 CAP critical values (Wilson 95% CI 91–100%; a shared safety floor, not a discriminating comparison); the engine produced identical top-10 lists and alerts in three repeated runs of every stay.

### 4.2 Intensive care (eICU-CRD demo)

In 2,314 adult ICU stays, using the first panel with at least eight analytes and no clinical text, 2,707 diagnoses were coded, of which 2,432 had their key analyte in the panel and are scored: 47.9% (stay-clustered 95% CI 45.9–49.9) appeared in the top-3, 60.8% (58.8–62.7) in the top-5 and 74.1% (72.3–75.8) in the top-10 of the flat differential; counting either tier, top-5 recall was 71.9% (Table 2). Recall was insensitive to the minimum panel size (60.8% at ≥ 8 analytes, 60.5% at ≥ 20, 59.8% at ≥ 25; Supplementary Table S6). Recall@5 was 88.1% for acute myocardial infarction, 82.8% for heart failure, 83.0% for anaemia, 77.5% for acute kidney injury and 77.9% for gastrointestinal bleeding, but 49.1% for sepsis, 45.1% for diabetes and 35.8% for CKD.

Recall is only one side of the list. At the label-group level the engine asserted a mean of 4.5 groups per stay within its top-5 and 8.0 across the full differential; precision@5 was 14.2% (95% CI 13.3–15.2), F1@5 23.1% and NDCG@5 56.5% (54.1–58.8). A prevalence-ranking baseline that simply lists the most frequent groups whose key analyte is present reached recall@5 51.3% and NDCG@5 39.0%, and at k = 10 its recall (76.8%) exceeded the engine's (74.1%), because ten slots cover almost every frequent group; the engine's advantage is therefore in ranking (NDCG, recall@3 47.9% vs 36.1%), not in list coverage at k = 10. Per-group PPV against the problem list ranged from 69% (diabetic ketoacidosis) to below 10% for findings that are rarely coded as diagnoses (hypocalcaemia, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis), a lower bound given problem-list incompleteness (Supplementary Table S11). Automated laboratory-criterion matching (not clinician adjudication) of the 10,380 top-5 label-group assertions (4.486 per stay × 2,314 stays; fixed single-analyte criteria written before scoring; Supplementary Table S11) found 14.2% on the problem list, a further 54.8% not coded but satisfying the group's laboratory criterion in the same panel (e.g. hypokalaemia with potassium < 3.5 mmol/L), 12.6% criterion-negative — the closest available estimate of true false positives — and 18.4% in groups without a single-analyte criterion. Precision@5 therefore lies between 14.2% (problem list) and 69.0% (problem list or laboratory criterion); the criterion-positive assertions are laboratory findings that were real but not charted as diagnoses, and whether they were clinically useful cannot be judged without a clinician. The reference standard bounds this metric (Fig. 3b; the 2,707 targets are the coded labels before the key-analyte filter). Of these, 541 belonged to groups with no single laboratory criterion, 275 lacked the key analyte in the first panel and 431 were contradicted by the first panel (for example hypokalaemia coded with potassium 4.1 mmol/L after repletion). Among the 1,460 labels whose first panel met the guideline criterion, flat recall was 29.6 / 62.5 / 78.0 / 91.8% at k = 1 / 3 / 5 / 10 and two-tier recall 43.0 / 77.7 / 89.2 / 97.2%. On this subset recall@5 exceeded 85% for renal impairment, acute kidney injury, anaemia, myocardial infarction, diabetic ketoacidosis, heart failure, hypokalaemia and pancreatitis; the only groups in which v7 lost against the product release were CKD (41.1% vs 74.4%, by design) and leukocytosis (46.9% vs 67.3%) (Supplementary Fig. S3).

### 4.5b Post-freeze development step (engine v8, registry 14.2.0): what the roadmap did and did not deliver

After the frozen v7 results above were complete, the twelve improvement proposals raised in review were each tested against the frozen v7 artefacts before any code was changed (Supplementary Note 7). Three were supported and implemented; the others were refuted by the data or require resources we do not have. (i) *Adaptive truncation of the differential to raise precision@5*: on the v7 top-5, every precision gain was paid for almost one-for-one in recall (fixed k = 5: precision 14.2%, recall 60.8%; score ≥ 0.6: 19.0% / 53.5%; score ≥ 0.7: 23.3% / 43.3%), and the precision ceiling under perfect ranking is about 21% because the problem list carries ~1.05 evaluable labels per stay; v8 therefore adds a descriptive `confidence_band` and a `concise` flag to each entry without changing the ranking. (ii) *Asymmetric suppression of a covering diagnosis (e.g. diabetes hiding ketoacidosis)*: of 107 coded ketoacidosis stays, 68 were in the top-5, 7 at ranks 6–10 and 26 absent; the absent stays lacked bicarbonate, anion gap or ketones in the panel (glucose 382–853 mg/dL with no bicarbonate), so the cause is a missing criterion, not suppression, and asserting ketoacidosis from glucose alone would contradict the ADA criteria. (iii) *Dropping the 28 rules whose source locator had been removed in 13.4.1*: these are toxicology, transfusion and endocrine thresholds (lithium, digoxin, salicylate, toxic alcohols, cyanide, amatoxin, β-blocker, TACO, valproate hyperammonaemia, central diabetes insipidus, pseudohyponatraemia, protein-losing enteropathy); ablation showed no effect only because eICU contains no such cases, so instead of deleting them registry 14.2.0 re-sources them with 13 Crossref-resolved DOIs (verified 2026-10-26), leaving zero rules without a locator and no logic changed. (iv) *Further derived indices*: v8 adds MELD/MELD-Na (computed only when bilirubin > 2 mg/dL or INR > 1.5; prognostic, never a diagnostic vote) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (informational); CURB-65, HAS-BLED, CHA₂DS₂-VASc and Child-Pugh need non-laboratory inputs and were not added. (v) *A supervised sepsis component*: because sepsis onset is an outcome rather than a laboratory criterion, we built a transparent companion risk model outside the untrained engine (L2 logistic regression and a regularised gradient-boosting reference over last-observation-carried-forward vitals and laboratory values, their 6- and 12-h trends, SIRS/qSOFA components and time since admission), trained once on PhysioNet set A and evaluated on the untouched sets B and B2. Hourly AUROC was 0.757 / 0.750 (boosting) and 0.717 / 0.726 (logistic); the score 6, 12 and 24 h before onset discriminated with AUROC 0.78 / 0.80 / 0.77 (B) and 0.77 / 0.77 / 0.79 (B2); the official PhysioNet utility at a threshold fixed on set A was 0.253 / 0.220 (challenge winner on the full hidden test: 0.36); and in the patient-level frame used for the engine the companion reached 0.654 / 0.631 against the engine's 0.610 / 0.588 (Supplementary Table S16). The gain is real but modest, and the module is labelled predictive, not diagnostic. The v8 development gate reproduced every v7 safety battery result and every eICU count exactly (flat recall@5 60.8%, two-tier@5 71.9%; 680 unit tests plus 4 new); v8 has not been through a quarantine round and no frozen number in this paper derives from it.

With the frozen engine v7 and stay-clustered inference, recall@5 — the pre-specified primary comparison — was 60.7% (56.5–65.0) for Hemoglyph versus 59.6% (55.3–63.6) for the LLM, a difference of +1.2 points (−2.5 to +5.1; cluster-adjusted McNemar p = 0.54). ### 4.7 Calibration and subgroups

Pooled over 52,689 stay–group units, AUROC was 0.797 but the raw scores were over-confident: calibration slope 0.25, calibration-in-the-large −2.50, ECE 0.162, Brier 0.117 (95% cluster-bootstrap CI 0.114–0.119), log-loss 0.379; per group the slope ranged from 0.13 (sepsis) to 0.49 (hyperkalaemia) and the Brier score from 0.029 (hyperkalaemia) to 0.492 (acute myocardial infarction, where any raised troponin raises the group score) (Fig. 8, Supplementary Table S8). Isotonic recalibration, fitted and evaluated on disjoint stay-level folds (split-half cross-fitting by stay: the map fitted on odd-indexed stays is applied to even-indexed stays and vice versa, so no unit is calibrated on itself), reduced ECE to 0.003 (Brier 0.040) within the same cohort; this demonstrates that the ranks are recalibrable, not that a transfer-ready calibration exists, because the map is learned on eICU and was not tested on an independent cohort and yielded positive net benefit over 'treat all' across thresholds of 0.05–0.60 for the five largest groups (Fig. 8). Raw scores are therefore ranks and are not displayed as probabilities in the interface; any deployment would require population-specific recalibration. Twenty-seven of the 29 groups (exceptions: malnutrition, n = 16, AUROC 0.515; hypocalcaemia, n = 10, 0.627) with ≥ 10 coded labels (five without a single-analyte criterion, hence absent from the 24-group mean AUROC of Table 5) had AUROCs above 0.5 after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (exceptions: malnutrition, hypocalcaemia). Recall@5 was 62.3% (59.5–64.9; 1,392 stays) in men and 58.5% (54.4–62.3; 922 stays) in women, 58–64% across age bands and 59–61% across the three largest ethnicity strata (Supplementary Table S4, with n and CI); the engine contains no race term and uses the race-free CKD-EPI 2021 equation.

### 4.8 Component ablation

Table 5 reports the frozen engine with one component at a time switched off or altered on the full 2,314-stay cohort (same stays, label map and scorer; engine file unchanged; environment switches and in-process patches only). 

Two components carry the signal. Removing the rule-engine vote from fusion cost 12.1 points of flat recall@5 (−14.4 to −9.8) and removing the expert syndrome layer alone cost 9.5 points (−11.1 to −7.9). The remaining layers contributed little on this task: the Bayesian network −1.3 points (−2.0 to −0.6; −3.7 points on the two-tier metric), the fuzzy and knowledge-graph layers and the risk models each within ±0.5 points (the risk-model layer changed no ranking at all), and the fusion refinements—Yager versus Dempster combination, discord shrinkage, single-source discount—within ±0.5 points; collapsing the fusion weights to uniform cost 1.4 points, shifting weight toward the rule layer (0.55) changed nothing (−0.0, −0.6 to +0.5) and shifting it away (0.20) cost 1.1 points (−1.8 to −0.3), so the hand-set weights are not a hidden tuning knob (Supplementary Table S12). The arm that bears on the provenance concern is the registry restriction: with only the 272 manually curated anchor rules executable and the 32,911 AI-assisted rules switched off, flat recall@5 fell by 0.9 points (−2.1 to +0.2), two-tier recall@5 by 3.0 points (−4.0 to −2.1), panel-supported recall@5 from 78.0% to 73.3%, and mean per-group AUROC from 0.767 to 0.732. On clinician-listed ICU diagnoses, therefore, the large AI-drafted rule base adds breadth and discrimination at the margin but is not what drives the headline recall; the hand-curated anchors and the syndrome layer are. Because the syndrome layer could mask the registry's contribution, we also ran the fourth cell of the 2 × 2 (anchor-only registry with the syndrome layer off): flat recall@5 was 48.2%, so the AI-drafted rules contributed 0.9 points when the syndrome layer was present but 3.1 points (51.3% → 48.2%) when it was absent, and the syndrome layer contributed 9.5 points with the full registry and 11.7 points with anchors only. The two components are therefore partly redundant rather than the bulk being inert; the conclusion that the headline rests on the curated anchors and the syndrome layer stands, with the qualification that the AI-drafted rules supply about three points of recall that the syndrome layer otherwise covers. Removing the 28 `unverified_source` rules did not change a single ranking. We regard this as reassuring for safety (the unaudited rules are not load-bearing) and as a limit on the claim that scale of the registry is itself an advantage; its value, if any, lies in coverage of rarer entities that the eICU label set does not test.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

Hemoglyph—a hybrid knowledge-based reasoning system with no training phase—recovered 61% of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses in five suggestions from laboratory values alone, 78% of those diagnoses that the first panel could actually support, and reproduced screening AUROCs of 0.92–0.99 across three independent NHANES cycles while asserting disease in 2 of 485 guideline-healthy adults (and in none of 1,000 synthetic normal panels). It was competitive with, and for guideline-defined conditions often superior to, supervised gradient boosting trained on the labels, and in a blinded paired comparison on identical inputs the frozen engine was equivalent to a unnamed frontier LLM on the pre-specified metric — ranking fewer diagnoses first but omitting fewer — while remaining deterministic and traceable to cited rules. Its performance was weaker for multifactorial syndromes without a laboratory definition—sepsis, heart failure without natriuretic peptides, gastrointestinal bleeding—and it failed, as pre-specified, to predict Sepsis-3 onset.

Three findings govern how these numbers should be read. First, the apparent ceiling near 60% is largely a property of the reference standard: a quarter of admission-level labels were not inferable from the first panel, and a supervised model trained on the labels themselves did not exceed 0.82 AUROC for outcome-defined groups. The panel-supported metric, though its thresholds were fixed in code before scoring, was defined after the ceiling was observed and is structurally optimistic; we present it beside, not instead of, the flat metric. Second, the untrained engine matched supervised models wherever a guideline definition exists and lost wherever the label is an outcome that the model learns from hidden correlates—the expected division of labour between knowledge and learning, which argues for hybrid rather than replacement designs. Third, the LLM was better at ranks 1–3 (recall@1 28.2% vs 24.0%) and worse at coverage (recall@10 65.1% vs 72.0%; it omitted 34.9% of labels against 21.8%), and its run-to-run and prompt-to-prompt variation (±5 points; test–retest κ 0.69) exceeded its difference from the engine. Per group (Fig. 7) the differences are small where a single analyte defines the label (renal impairment 83% vs 81%, acute kidney injury 76% vs 80%, sepsis 45% vs 42%) and large where a multi-analyte pattern must be assembled (engine ahead for gastrointestinal bleeding 84% vs 50%, diabetes 50% vs 37%, hypokalaemia 59% vs 41%) or where the engine's covering-diagnosis suppression demotes a finding once its cause is named (LLM ahead for metabolic acidosis 79% vs 50%, hyperkalaemia 90% vs 70%, DKA 83% vs 73%); the two systems make different errors, which argues for measuring them jointly rather than declaring a winner. Fourth, the ablation (Section 4.8) locates the result in the rule-engine vote (−12.1 points when removed) and the ~900 hand-curated syndrome constellations (−9.5 points; these were written by the first author from guideline criteria and, like the registry, have not been independently validated). The system is therefore best described as guideline-*transcribed* rather than guideline-derived: its knowledge was transcribed from guidelines by a non-clinician author with AI assistance, the components that carry the result are precisely those no independent audit has examined, and the result measures what that transcription achieves rather than what the guidelines would achieve in expert hands, not in the 33,183-rule registry: with the 32,911 AI-drafted rules switched off, flat recall@5 moved by 0.9 points. This limits the influence of the unaudited rules on what we report, but it also removes registry scale as a merit of the system, and it shows that the Bayesian, fuzzy, knowledge-graph and risk-model layers were close to inert on this cohort (the risk-model layer changed no ranking). We report this rather than assert that every layer is necessary; a leaner engine would have produced the same headline numbers. Fifth, a prevalence-ranking baseline is a sobering comparator: it reaches recall@5 of 51% and overtakes the engine at k = 10, because ten slots cover nearly every common ICU group. The engine's measurable contribution is therefore the ordering (NDCG@5 56.5% vs 39.0%; recall@3 47.9% vs 36.1%) and the per-stay specificity of what it asserts (precision@5 14.2% vs 10.8%), not list coverage, and precision against an incomplete problem list remains low in absolute terms.

### 5.2 Comparison with prior work

Early CDSS such as CASNET could not distinguish lack of evidence from contradictory evidence; DST-based approaches addressed this gap but, through Dempster's normalisation, can become confident in a wrong hypothesis under severe conflict. Hemoglyph adopts Yager's combination within binary frames, assigning conflict mass to ignorance and treating abstention as a first-class state; the empirical consequence is visible in the healthy-adult sweeps and in a false-assertion proxy of 8% in stays with an empty problem list, and the ablation with Dempster normalisation in place of Yager's rule quantifies what the choice contributes. Unlike architectures that use an LLM to generate explanations (Ashworth et al., 2025), the evaluated Hemoglyph release uses no language model at inference time, so hallucination is excluded by construction rather than mitigated. Unlike supervised models that require labelled data (Rank et al., 2020; Fleuren et al., 2020), it is transparent and portable across units and populations, which the unit-twin battery and the SI-based NHANES, UCI and HCV cohorts demonstrate. Compared with Isabel and DXplain, which take symptoms and have been evaluated on 36–201 cases (Bridges, 2024, 2025; MGB, 2025; Bond et al., 2012), this study isolates laboratory-only reasoning on more than 20,000 real encounters, pre-specifies its analysis internally and releases its knowledge base under an open licence into an ecosystem in which public executable guideline repositories have been withdrawn.

### 5.3 Clinical implications

Hemoglyph is a fully offline, deterministic and explainable system: it operates locally with zero telemetry and therefore protects patient privacy by design. Its abstention mechanism is clinically important: when evidence is insufficient or inconsistent, the system declares uncertainty rather than issuing a definitive and potentially hazardous diagnosis—valuable when laboratory results are influenced by physiological confounders or pre-analytical error. Its natural positions are the post-analytical step of a laboratory information system—interpretive comments and critical-pattern alerts generated locally—and a reproducible, auditable baseline against which LLM-based interpretation can be measured. Hemoglyph is a research prototype: it has no regulatory clearance, has not been evaluated prospectively or with clinicians in the loop, and must not be used for patient care. Clinicians should note that raw scores are ranks (calibration slope 0.25): thresholds must be calibrated to the target population (as the ALT task illustrates) and the interface does not present scores as probabilities. Two human-factors risks apply to any such tool and are untested here: automation bias (acceptance of a plausible but wrong ranked list) and alert fatigue from a 25-entry differential; the planned reader study measures the first. A rule base of 33,183 entries also carries a maintenance cost — guideline updates must be re-encoded and re-gated — which the versioned registry and regression gate are designed to make tractable but do not make free. The two architectures could also be combined: a language model to extract and normalise free-text results—the step at which our automatic parser lost half of the PMC cases—and a rule engine to reason over them.

### 5.4 What changed since the earlier version of the system

Relative to the earlier description of Hemoglyph (registry 13.1.0, engine v1), the present work adds: (i) a pre-specified SAP with fifteen dated amendments, SHA-256 freezes and two one-shot quarantine rounds, including a failed round; (ii) the expert syndrome layer, causal attribution and guideline-semantics guards of engine v6–v7, which traded about three points of flat recall for correctness (a single creatinine no longer asserts CKD; a normal leukocyte count no longer yields a syndrome) and which we disclose as a deliberate trade; (iii) registry curation from 13.1.0 to 14.1.0 with 1,487 retired records, a provenance audit and `unverified_source` flags; (iv) the panel-supported metric that quantifies the reference-standard ceiling; (v) comparators on identical inputs—raw thresholds, supervised models, registry ablation and a blinded unnamed frontier LLM with test–retest; (vi) three NHANES cycles instead of one, three sepsis sets, MIMIC-IV demo and PMC case reports; (vii) calibration, decision-curve, subgroup and next-test analyses; and (viii) open licensing of engine (AGPL-3.0) and registries (CC BY-SA 4.0). The leukocytosis pattern that could not be diagnosed in version v1 is now recognised (57.9% recall@5 with the registry on).
A post-freeze analysis of twelve reviewer-suggested improvements (Supplementary Note 7) found that the two that promised the largest gains—truncating the differential to raise precision, and suppressing covering diagnoses—would have traded recall for precision one-for-one or asserted diagnoses without their guideline criteria; the remaining recall ceiling on eICU is set by panels that lack the defining analyte and by the problem-list reference standard rather than by the reasoning layer, which is why we moved the supervised sepsis component outside the engine instead of training the engine.

### 5.5 Limitations

This study has significant limitations. The components that carry the result—272 anchor rules and ~900 syndrome constellations—were written by a non-clinician first author, who also holds a financial interest in the commercial product, and have not been independently audited; the LLM arm is not reproducible against a named model; the panel-supported metric is post hoc; the Bayesian priors and likelihood ratios are hand-coded without a per-value citation table; and no version-control history exists for the development period (a retrospective hash manifest is given in Supplementary Note 6), so the statement that no change was outcome-fitted rests on the dated change log alone. First, no prospective or clinician-in-the-loop evaluation has been conducted; all evidence is retrospective, and a pre-registered crossover reader study with an automation-bias endpoint is in preparation. Second, of 33,183 executable rules only 272 (0.8%) are manually curated guideline anchors; the remaining 32,911 (32,898 candidates plus 13 demoted former anchors) were generated with AI assistance and remain unaudited (status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`; the 13 demoted anchors keep their anchor provenance but no longer carry anchor weight); 34 sources lack a locator. An independent audit of a stratified random sample of rules by two clinical specialists has been designed (sampling frame and forms in `eval/human/`) but not yet performed; until it is, the knowledge base should be regarded as AI-drafted and automatically audited, not expert-validated. Third, the registries were developed by the first author with the assistance of a large language model and have not been reviewed by an independent expert panel; the label-map audit (400 stays, precision 100%, sensitivity 98.3%) was likewise performed by the first author. Also, the quarantine criteria were changed after Round 1; Round 2 is supportive, not confirmatory, and the statistical co-author who reviewed the amendments is not independent of the study. Fourth, only the open demo subsets of eICU-CRD and MIMIC-IV were used, and their reference standards—admission-level problem lists and ICD codes—are not adjudicated panel-level diagnoses; the panel-supported metric is structurally optimistic. Fifth, the LLM comparison is an author-run self-baseline with one model class, two prompts, one run per prompt plus one re-read, no retrieval augmentation, regex scoring, and possible pre-training exposure of the public eICU demo (which would favour the LLM). Sixth, laboratory-defined NHANES labels overlap the inputs; only the self-reported labels are independent. Seventh, raw scores are uncalibrated, and sample sizes in UCI HCV and ILPD are small. Eighth, performance on multifactorial syndromes is weak and the engine does not predict sepsis. Ninth, Iranian populations and non-English laboratory conventions were not evaluated, Engine v8 and the sepsis companion are post-freeze development work: they have not been through a quarantine round, the companion's boosting model was regularised once after a look at set B, and none of the frozen numbers in this paper derive from them, and the OCR layer and consistency solver were not assessed at all; the system's claims are limited to structured values. Tenth, the eICU and MIMIC demo subsets are not representative of their parent databases, and first panels with fewer than eight analytes (198 stays) were excluded. Finally, the first author is a secondary-school student and the system and analyses were developed with AI-agent assistance, declared below.

### 5.6 Conclusion

An open, offline, knowledge-driven hybrid reasoning system — a research prototype, not a clinical device — can interpret structured laboratory panels to the ceiling that the first panel allows, reproduce population-screening performance across independent survey cycles, and perform on a par with a frontier LLM in a blinded paired test while remaining deterministic, abstention-aware and auditable—under internally pre-specified criteria that were weakened from superiority to non-inferiority after a failed first quarantine round. Its limits — outcome-defined syndromes, over-confident raw scores, an AI-drafted rule base awaiting independent expert audit and the absence of any prospective evaluation — are stated so that they can be measured and closed.
