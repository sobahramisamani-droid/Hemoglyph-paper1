# An untrained, auditable, locally run, AI-drafted guideline-sourced rule engine for structured laboratory-panel interpretation: pre-specified evaluation on public datasets (eICU and MIMIC-IV demo subsets, NHANES, PhysioNet) with frozen quarantine rounds, component ablation and a blinded comparison against an unnamed frontier language model

## Abstract

Laboratory interpretation remains largely manual; large language models (LLMs) interpret panels fluently but neither reproducibly nor locally. Hemoglyph is a research-prototype offline engine that ranks a cited differential from a structured laboratory panel using 33,183 executable rules transcribed from guidelines—99.2% AI-drafted by a non-clinician author and not yet clinically audited—without outcome training. All results concern structured analyte–value pairs; the OCR front end was not evaluated. Under an internal, hash-frozen analysis plan (not publicly registered) with two one-shot quarantine rounds—the first failed its superiority criteria, which were then changed to non-inferiority, so the second round is supportive, not confirmatory—we evaluated the frozen engine on the eICU demo (2,314 ICU stays), MIMIC-IV demo, 9,000 NHANES adults, 14,420 sepsis patients and external cohorts. From laboratory values alone, 60.8% (95% CI clustered by stay 58.7–62.9) of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses appeared in the top-5, at precision@5 14.2% against an incomplete problem list; a prevalence baseline reached 51.3% and overtook the engine at k = 10, so the engine's contribution is ordering (NDCG@5 56.5% vs 39.0%), not coverage. Screening AUROCs reproduced across three NHANES cycles (0.88–0.99 for six targets; 0.87–0.88 for ALT elevation) with 2 false assertions among 485 healthy adults. Blinded on identical inputs, the engine and an unnamed frontier LLM (agent platform; model not disclosed) were at parity on recall@5 (60.7% vs 59.6%, p = 0.54); the LLM led at rank 1, omitted more labels (34.9% vs 21.8%) and was only moderately self-consistent (κ 0.69); the engine is deterministic. Sepsis AUROC was 0.60 (pre-specified negative result; SIRS/qSOFA rules added afterwards were validated once). Ablation located the result in the 272 hand-curated anchors and the syndrome layer—the components not yet independently audited—with the AI-drafted bulk adding 0.9 points (3.1 without the syndrome layer); correctness fixes cost three recall points versus the product release. Iranian populations were not evaluated. Code and rules are public for audit.

## Introduction

Roughly 70 % of clinical decisions draw on laboratory results, yet interpretive comments accompany only a small fraction of reports, and post-analytical errors—missed critical values, patterns that span several analytes, unit confusion—remain a leading source of diagnostic harm [1–3]. Three families of software address this gap. Rule-based expert systems embedded in laboratory information systems are transparent but typically narrow, hand-maintained and rarely evaluated outside their home institution [4,5]. Supervised machine-learning models discriminate specific targets well but need labelled training data, degrade under distribution shift and cannot be audited clause by clause [6,7]. Most recently, large language models (LLMs) have produced fluent interpretations of laboratory reports, but their outputs vary between runs, generally cannot run on commodity laboratory hardware without transmitting patient data, fabricate references at non-trivial rates and misclassify values as high or low even when reference ranges are supplied [8–11].

A fourth option—an engine that interprets whole panels from an explicit, versioned knowledge base, runs offline and emits a ranked, explainable differential—has seldom been evaluated at scale. The barrier is methodological rather than technical: such engines are usually validated on vignettes written by their own authors, and the few real-data evaluations use a single institution and no comparator [12]. Mature diagnostic decision-support systems (Isabel, DXplain) have recently been compared with GPT-4 and successor models and found within a few percentage points on recall while being reproducible and free of fabricated references [13–16]; none isolated laboratory-only reasoning. At the same time, the public infrastructure for executable guideline knowledge has contracted: the AHRQ CDS Connect repository and authoring tool went offline in April 2025 (the artefacts continue as an HL7 community edition), and a dated search (2026-10-24) of AHRQ CDS Connect, ECRI Guidelines Trust, NICE, WHO SMART guideline kits, OpenCDS/Arden collections and PubMed found no openly licensed, machine-executable rule set specific to laboratory-panel interpretation (method in Supplementary Note 5). We restrict the engine and its evaluation to structured laboratory values by design: it isolates what can be inferred from the panel itself, the one input every laboratory report shares, and avoids measuring the transcription of a diagnosis already written in the history.

Here we report the design and a pre-specified, multi-dataset retrospective evaluation of Hemoglyph. Every result concerns the inference engine fed with structured analyte–value pairs; the optical-character-recognition front end of the desktop product was not evaluated, and a free-text parser tested on case reports performed poorly, so the figures below should not be read as end-to-end performance from a scanned report. Our aims were (i) to describe an architecture that keeps every output traceable to a cited rule; (ii) to evaluate the frozen engine against clinician- and guideline-derived reference standards in public datasets spanning intensive care, community survey, referral laboratory, hepatology and case reports; (iii) to compare the untrained engine with a single-analyte threshold, with supervised models trained on the same inputs and—blinded, on identical inputs—with a contemporary LLM; (iv) to quantify the ceiling imposed by the reference standard itself; and (v) to report failures transparently, including a quarantine round that failed and a pre-specified negative result. The inference engine evaluated here is the same single source file (`analyse_gidlines.py`, engine v7) that ships inside a proprietary desktop product owned by the first author; this financial interest is declared in full under Competing interests and motivated the frozen, one-shot design. We frame this as an engineering and retrospective evaluation study of a research prototype, not a claim of clinical validation; the optical-character-recognition front end of the desktop product was not evaluated and all inputs were structured values.

## Results

### System and knowledge base

Hemoglyph is a single-process Python engine (Fig. 1). Inputs are analyte–value pairs with units plus age, sex and encounter type; clinical context and serial values are optional. After UCUM-based unit normalisation and derivation of 100 indices (CKD-EPI 2021 eGFR, corrected anion gap, FIB-4 and others), a rule layer evaluates 33,183 executable rules (registry 14.1.0; 272 curated anchors and 32,911 AI-drafted, unaudited candidates) derived from 785 guideline sources; a curated expert syndrome layer (~900 multi-analyte constellations with tiered anchor strengths and context guards) adjusts and guards; a fixed fusion step combines rule votes, Bayesian priors and fuzzy degrees; and a ranking-hygiene step applies causal attribution (a covering diagnosis explains its downstream findings), KDIGO chronicity rules and the universal definition of myocardial infarction. Output is a two-tier list—specific diagnoses and umbrella syndromes—each with `explained_by` findings, an evidence state and suggested next tests. The engine is deterministic (byte-identical output in 50/50 repeated stays), runs on a single CPU core without GPU in ~1 s per panel (median 0.4–0.5 s, p95 0.9 s in a 2-GB sandbox) and makes no network calls. What is open and what is proprietary is tabulated in Supplementary Table S15 (engine source, registries, evaluation code and frozen outputs open; desktop shell, licensing and OCR path proprietary; only the open components were evaluated). The desktop product around it is proprietary and node-locked; its optional cloud OCR path transmits report images and is outside the offline claim, which applies to the engine evaluated here.

The registry was constructed with AI assistance from guideline text (99.2% of executable rules; 272 anchor rules were hand-curated by the first author, who is not a clinician) and filtered by automated consistency audits; it has not been audited rule-by-rule by clinicians, and the per-rule error rate is therefore unknown. Of 34,673 records, 1,487 are retired and 3 lack an executable expression; the 33,183 executable rules comprise 272 curated anchors, 13 demoted former anchors and 32,898 candidates (Supplementary Table S10 reconciles all counts against the live file; 388 of the 785 listed sources are cited by an executable rule). Each output entry displays its rule's status and evidence tier, and both are machine-readable fields in the registry. A provenance audit resolved 112 DOIs via Crossref and reached 533 landing pages; 39 sources were unreachable from our sandbox, 34 lacked a locator, nine invalid DOIs were removed and 28 rules were flagged `unverified_source` (Supplementary Fig. S1).

### Pre-specification, freezes and quarantine

Evaluation followed an internal statistical analysis plan with 15 dated amendments (Supplementary Note 1). The plan was time-stamped and hash-frozen inside the project repository but was not deposited in a public registry; we therefore write 'pre-specified', not 'pre-registered', throughout. Before each quarantine round the code and registry were frozen with a SHA-256 manifest (261 and 316 files). Quarantine data had never been run before the freeze and were run once; the only exception is the MIMIC-IV demo, which was re-run post hoc with the frozen v7 for comparability and is labelled as such. Round 1 (MIMIC-IV demo, NHANES 2013–14, PhysioNet sepsis set B) failed two primary criteria that had been written as superiority over the prior release; the cause was that guideline-correctness changes (e.g. a single creatinine no longer asserts chronic kidney disease) lowered label recall for CKD. We report that failure, re-specified the criteria as non-inferiority with dated justification (Amendment 15, written after Round 1 was known and before any Round-2 file was opened: NHANES AUROC within 0.03 of the prior release on six targets—diabetes, CKD, anaemia, iron deficiency, hypercholesterolaemia, hyperuricaemia; ALT elevation was excluded a priori because its label was known to be definition-sensitive, and its non-inferiority is reported post hoc (0.877 vs 0.880, within 0.03)—with ≤ 1% healthy false assertions; sepsis AUROC not more than 0.02 below; no new safety-battery failure), implemented six further correctness changes (engine v7) and ran Round 2 (NHANES 2015–16, sepsis set B2, new healthy sweep), which met the re-specified criteria (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S2). Because the criteria changed between rounds, Round 2 should be read as supportive evidence of non-regression, not as the confirmatory test originally planned. All results below are for the frozen v7/14.1.0 release unless stated.

### Intensive care (eICU-CRD demo)

In 2,314 adult ICU stays from 186 US hospitals, using the first panel with ≥ 8 analytes and no clinical text, 47.9% (stay-clustered 95% CI 45.8–50.0) of 2,432 clinician-listed, laboratory-relevant diagnoses (2,707 coded; 275 lacked the key analyte and are reported as 'analyte missing') appeared in the top-3, 60.8% (58.7–62.9) in the top-5 and 74.1% (72.2–76.0) in the top-10 of the flat differential (Table 2). Raising the analyte threshold from ≥ 8 to ≥ 25 changed top-5 recall by ≤ 1 point (60.8% → 59.8%). Counting either tier, top-5 recall was 71.9%. The engine asserted a mean of 4.5 label groups per stay in its top-5; precision@5 was 14.2% (13.3–15.2), F1@5 23.1% and NDCG@5 56.5% (54.1–58.8) against an incomplete problem list (lower bounds). 

### Prevalence baseline

A prevalence-ranking baseline (most frequent groups whose key analyte is present) reached recall@5 51.3% and NDCG@5 39.0%; at k = 10 its recall (76.8%) exceeded the engine's 74.1%, so the engine's advantage lies in ranking, not list coverage (Supplementary Table S11). Automated laboratory-criterion matching of the 10,380 top-5 label-group assertions (4.486 assertions per stay × 2,314 stays; fixed single-analyte criteria written before scoring, not clinician adjudication; Supplementary Table S11) found 14.2% on the problem list, a further 54.8% not coded but satisfying the group's laboratory criterion in the same panel (e.g. hypokalaemia with potassium < 3.5 mmol/L), 12.6% criterion-negative — the closest available estimate of true false positives — and 18.4% in groups without a single-analyte criterion. Precision@5 therefore lies between 14.2% (problem list) and 69.0% (problem list or laboratory criterion); the criterion-positive assertions are laboratory findings that were real but not charted as diagnoses, and whether they were clinically useful cannot be judged without a clinician. Recall@5 was 88.1% for acute myocardial infarction, 82.8% for heart failure, 83.0% for anaemia, 77.5% for acute kidney injury and 77.9% for gastrointestinal bleeding, but 49.1% for sepsis, 45.1% for diabetes and 35.8% for CKD.

The reference standard bounds this metric (Fig. 2b). Of 2,707 label targets, 541 belonged to groups with no single laboratory criterion, 275 lacked the key analyte in the first panel and 431 were contradicted by the first panel (e.g. hypokalaemia coded with potassium 4.1 mmol/L after repletion). Among the 1,460 labels whose first panel met the guideline criterion—a secondary, post-hoc metric whose thresholds were fixed before scoring but whose definition followed the observation of the ceiling—flat recall was 29.6/62.5/78.0/91.8% at k = 1/3/5/10 and two-tier recall 43.0/77.7/89.2/97.2%. Per-group recall on this subset exceeded 85% for renal impairment, AKI, anaemia, AMI, DKA, heart failure, hypokalaemia and pancreatitis; the only groups where v7 lost against the previous release were CKD (41.1% vs 74.4%, by design) and leukocytosis (46.9% vs 67.3%) (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Three comparators used identical inputs (Fig. 3). A raw key-analyte threshold matched or exceeded the engine's AUROC for single-analyte groups (hyperkalaemia 0.96 vs 0.92; anaemia 0.83 vs 0.78), as expected for findings that are definitions. Gradient boosting and logistic regression trained on the labels (five-fold out-of-fold) exceeded the untrained engine only in outcome-defined groups without a laboratory signature—sepsis (0.82 vs 0.68), GI bleeding (0.85 vs 0.75), alcohol withdrawal (0.80 vs 0.54)—and were matched or beaten in nine guideline-defined groups (CKD 0.79 vs 0.81; hyponatraemia 0.85 vs 0.89; thrombocytopenia 0.85 vs 0.89; cirrhosis 0.83 vs 0.87). A component ablation of the frozen engine is reported below.

Adding documented history and medications raised recall@5 from 58.2% to 59.1% for labels not named in the history (n = 2,467) but from 49.6% to 72.9% for labels already named (n = 240); the context arm therefore mainly echoes documentation and is reported only in the Supplement.

### Component ablation

Two components carry the signal. Removing the rule-engine vote from fusion cost 12.1 points of flat recall@5 (−14.4 to −9.8) and removing the expert syndrome layer alone cost 9.5 points (−11.1 to −7.9). The remaining layers contributed little on this task: the Bayesian network −1.3 points (−2.0 to −0.6; −3.7 points on the two-tier metric), the fuzzy and knowledge-graph layers and the risk models each within ±0.5 points (the risk-model layer changed no ranking at all), and the fusion refinements—Yager versus Dempster combination, discord shrinkage, single-source discount—within ±0.5 points; collapsing the fusion weights to uniform cost 1.4 points, shifting weight toward the rule layer (0.55) changed nothing (−0.0, −0.6 to +0.5) and shifting it away (0.20) cost 1.1 points (−1.8 to −0.3), so the hand-set weights are not a hidden tuning knob (Supplementary Table S12). Restricting the registry to the 272 manually curated anchor rules (32,911 AI-assisted rules off) lowered flat recall@5 by 0.9 points (−2.1 to +0.2), two-tier recall@5 by 3.0 points and mean per-group AUROC from 0.767 to 0.732 (Table 5): the AI-drafted bulk of the registry is not load-bearing for the headline result, which is reassuring for safety but limits any claim that registry scale is itself an advantage. Because the syndrome layer could mask the registry's contribution, we also ran the fourth cell of the 2 × 2 (anchor-only registry with the syndrome layer off): flat recall@5 was 48.2%, so the AI-drafted rules contributed 0.9 points when the syndrome layer was present but 3.1 points (51.3% → 48.2%) when it was absent, and the syndrome layer contributed 9.5 points with the full registry and 11.7 points with anchors only. The two components are therefore partly redundant rather than the bulk being inert; the conclusion that the headline rests on the curated anchors and the syndrome layer stands, with the qualification that the AI-drafted rules supply about three points of recall that the syndrome layer otherwise covers.

### Community survey: three independent NHANES cycles

In 3,000 adults per cycle, unweighted AUROCs reproduced across the development cycle (2017–18) and the two quarantine cycles (2013–14, 2015–16): diabetes 0.946/0.938/0.924, CKD 0.959/0.968/0.962, anaemia 0.972/0.994/0.983, iron deficiency 0.989/–/0.972, hyperuricaemia 0.943/0.958/0.955, hypercholesterolaemia 0.905/0.883/0.886 and ALT elevation 0.872/0.880/0.877 (Fig. 4; Table 3). ALT elevation is the seventh target (excluded a priori from the Amendment-15 criterion) and the weakest; it is reported in full rather than omitted. A correction: the previous version of this manuscript quoted 0.721 for 2017–18 and attributed it to a label-threshold change; on re-inspection that value came from a run of registry 14.0.0, not the frozen v7, and the label had not changed. All 2017–18 values above are from the v7 re-run (weighted 0.872/0.879/0.870, Supplementary Table S7). These laboratory-defined labels overlap the inputs and test criterion recognition; against self-reported physician diagnosis, which is independent of the inputs, AUROC was 0.90–0.92 for diabetes and 0.75–0.82 for kidney disease. Re-weighting with the NHANES examination weights changed laboratory-defined AUROCs by ≤ 0.015 (e.g. diabetes 0.951/0.939/0.923, CKD 0.947/0.967/0.958 for 2017–18/2013–14/2015–16) but lowered self-reported kidney disease in 2017–18 from 0.816 to 0.744 (0.693–0.794) (Supplementary Table S7). Among guideline-healthy adults (all of: HbA1c < 5.7%, fasting glucose < 100 mg/dL, eGFR ≥ 90, ACR < 30 mg/g, haemoglobin ≥ 12/13 g/dL, MCV 80–100 fL, ferritin ≥ 30 µg/L where measured, total cholesterol < 200 and triglycerides < 150 mg/dL, urate ≤ 6/7 mg/dL and ALT ≤ 25/33 U/L for women/men, sodium 135–145 mmol/L; full 30-analyte list in Supplementary Note 5—not all 2,045 analytes), the engine asserted a disease in 1/160, 1/246 and 0/79 (2/485, 0.4%). The filter covers 30 analytes, so a false assertion driven by an analyte outside it (e.g. a mildly abnormal result in an unfiltered analyte) is counted as a true finding rather than an error; the figure is therefore a lower bound on false assertions. Separately, 0/1,000 synthetic guideline-normal panels produced a disease; these are generated, not real, and are reported as a regression check, not as evidence.

### External cohorts and case reports

On the UCI thyroid referral set (7,743 records with TSH, of which 6,932 were scored—655 hypothyroid, 195 hyperthyroid, 6,082 controls—and 811 belonged to other diagnostic classes outside the two tasks) AUROC was 0.979 (0.974–0.983) for hypothyroidism (sensitivity 0.969, specificity 0.951) and 0.798 (0.783–0.814) for hyperthyroidism; on the HCV cohort (n = 615; 75 with liver disease, 30 cirrhosis) 0.925 (0.881–0.962) for any liver disease and 0.909 (0.837–0.969) for cirrhosis; on ILPD (n = 583; 416 patients) 0.704 (0.661–0.747). In MIMIC-IV demo (235 admissions, ICD-coded reference) top-5/10 recall was 47.9/61.7% for engine v6 in quarantine round 1 and 47.5/62.1% when the same admissions were re-run post hoc with the frozen v7 (no longer a one-shot result). On 50 PMC case reports given only the laboratory paragraph, the correct specific diagnosis was in the top-5 in 26% and the correct syndrome in 68% (automatic parsing: 10% and 44%), a lower bound without history (Table 3).

### Pre-specified negative result: sepsis

In PhysioNet/CinC 2019, the engine's sepsis score from first-24-h laboratory values discriminated Sepsis-3 onset with AUROC 0.600 (95% CI 0.575–0.625) in development set A and 0.610 (0.583–0.640) in quarantine set B (both engine v6.1/registry 14.1.0), and 0.588 (0.559–0.616) in quarantine set B2 with the frozen v7, versus 0.691, 0.712 and 0.679 for a supervised logistic model on the same features. A post-hoc concurrent analysis (labs and vital signs within ±6 h of onset; Amendment 13) reached 0.635 (0.597–0.673) versus 0.726 (Fig. 5). Guideline-sourced SIRS/qSOFA syndrome rules were added under Amendment 15 **after** the pre-specified negative result on sets A and B, developed on set A and validated once on B2, where they added about 0.04 AUROC; this is a post-hoc addition and is labelled as such. Hemoglyph is not a sepsis predictor.

### Post-freeze development step (engine v8, registry 14.2.0): what the roadmap did and did not deliver

After the frozen v7 results above were complete, the twelve improvement proposals raised in review were each tested against the frozen v7 artefacts before any code was changed (Supplementary Note 7). Three were supported and implemented; the others were refuted by the data or require resources we do not have. (i) *Adaptive truncation of the differential to raise precision@5*: on the v7 top-5, every precision gain was paid for almost one-for-one in recall (fixed k = 5: precision 14.2%, recall 60.8%; score ≥ 0.6: 19.0% / 53.5%; score ≥ 0.7: 23.3% / 43.3%), and the precision ceiling under perfect ranking is about 21% because the problem list carries ~1.05 evaluable labels per stay; v8 therefore adds a descriptive `confidence_band` and a `concise` flag to each entry without changing the ranking. (ii) *Asymmetric suppression of a covering diagnosis (e.g. diabetes hiding ketoacidosis)*: of 107 coded ketoacidosis stays, 68 were in the top-5, 7 at ranks 6–10 and 26 absent; the absent stays lacked bicarbonate, anion gap or ketones in the panel (glucose 382–853 mg/dL with no bicarbonate), so the cause is a missing criterion, not suppression, and asserting ketoacidosis from glucose alone would contradict the ADA criteria. (iii) *Dropping the 28 rules whose source locator had been removed in 13.4.1*: these are toxicology, transfusion and endocrine thresholds (lithium, digoxin, salicylate, toxic alcohols, cyanide, amatoxin, β-blocker, TACO, valproate hyperammonaemia, central diabetes insipidus, pseudohyponatraemia, protein-losing enteropathy); ablation showed no effect only because eICU contains no such cases, so instead of deleting them registry 14.2.0 re-sources them with 13 Crossref-resolved DOIs (verified 2026-10-26), leaving zero rules without a locator and no logic changed. (iv) *Further derived indices*: v8 adds MELD/MELD-Na (computed only when bilirubin > 2 mg/dL or INR > 1.5; prognostic, never a diagnostic vote) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (informational); CURB-65, HAS-BLED, CHA₂DS₂-VASc and Child-Pugh need non-laboratory inputs and were not added. (v) *A supervised sepsis component*: because sepsis onset is an outcome rather than a laboratory criterion, we built a transparent companion risk model outside the untrained engine (L2 logistic regression and a regularised gradient-boosting reference over last-observation-carried-forward vitals and laboratory values, their 6- and 12-h trends, SIRS/qSOFA components and time since admission), trained once on PhysioNet set A and evaluated on the untouched sets B and B2. Hourly AUROC was 0.757 / 0.750 (boosting) and 0.717 / 0.726 (logistic); the score 6, 12 and 24 h before onset discriminated with AUROC 0.78 / 0.80 / 0.77 (B) and 0.77 / 0.77 / 0.79 (B2); the official PhysioNet utility at a threshold fixed on set A was 0.253 / 0.220 (challenge winner on the full hidden test: 0.36); and in the patient-level frame used for the engine the companion reached 0.654 / 0.631 against the engine's 0.610 / 0.588 (Supplementary Table S16). The gain is real but modest, and the module is labelled predictive, not diagnostic. The v8 development gate reproduced every v7 safety battery result and every eICU count exactly (flat recall@5 60.8%, two-tier@5 71.9%; 680 unit tests plus 4 new); v8 has not been through a quarantine round and no frozen number in this paper derives from it.

### Blinded comparison with a unnamed frontier LLM LLM

On 400 stays (291 with scorable labels; 596 labels) an unnamed frontier LLM (accessed through an agent platform that does not disclose the model; see Methods) received the identical panel, age and sex and returned ten ranked diagnoses, blinded to labels (full protocol, prompts and contamination analysis in Supplementary Note 2). Scored against the frozen engine v7 with stay-clustered inference, recall@5—the pre-specified primary comparison—was 60.7% (56.5–65.0) for Hemoglyph versus 59.6% (55.3–63.6) for the LLM, a difference of +1.2 points (−2.5 to +5.1; cluster-adjusted McNemar p = 0.54). The LLM was better at rank 1 (28.2% vs 24.0%, p = 0.013) and rank 3 (50.8% vs 47.0%, p = 0.041); the engine was better at rank 10 (72.0% vs 65.1%, p = 0.0002) and left fewer labels absent (21.8% vs 34.9%); mean reciprocal rank 0.391 vs 0.408 (difference −0.017, −0.043 to +0.008) (Fig. 6; Table 4). Crediting either tier of the engine's output—a secondary, post-hoc metric with no LLM counterpart, since the LLM returns a single flat list that is simply reused—gave 69.8% vs 59.6%. The earlier comparison with engine v5.1 had favoured Hemoglyph (64.4% vs 59.6%, p = 0.009); the v7 correctness changes removed that advantage. The engine was better for GI bleeding (84 vs 50%), diabetes (50 vs 37%) and hypokalaemia (59 vs 41%); the LLM for metabolic acidosis (79 vs 50%), hyperkalaemia (90 vs 70%) and DKA (83 vs 73%). On 40 re-read panels the LLM's label-level agreement with itself was 85.7% (κ = 0.69) and with an alternative prompt 83.5–89.0%; its recall@5 varied by ±5 points across reads and prompts, more than the engine–LLM difference. The engine is deterministic. Both flagged 39/39 CAP critical values in 50 stays (Wilson 95% CI 91–100%; thresholds in Supplementary Note 5)—a shared safety floor, not a discriminating comparison.

### Calibration and subgroups

Pooled over 52,689 stay–group units, AUROC was 0.797 but raw scores were over-confident: calibration slope 0.25, calibration-in-the-large −2.50, ECE 0.162, Brier 0.117 (cluster-bootstrap CI 0.114–0.119), log-loss 0.379 (per-group slope 0.13–0.49; Fig. 7). Split-half cross-fitted isotonic recalibration (fitted on odd-indexed stays, applied to even-indexed and vice versa) reduced ECE to 0.003 (Brier 0.040) within eICU—evidence that the ranks are recalibrable, not a transferable calibration and gave positive net benefit over 'treat all' at thresholds of 0.05–0.60 for the five largest groups. Raw scores are therefore ranks and are not displayed as probabilities; deployment would require population-specific recalibration. Twenty-seven of the 29 groups with ≥ 10 coded labels (five of them without a single-analyte criterion, hence absent from the 24-group mean AUROC of Table 5) had per-group AUROCs above 0.5 after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment; the two exceptions were malnutrition (n = 16, AUROC 0.515) and hypocalcaemia (n = 10, 0.627), both small groups without a dedicated single-analyte signal in the first panel. Recall@5 was 62.3% (59.5–64.9; 1,392 stays) in men and 58.5% (54.4–62.3; 922 stays) in women, 58–64% across age bands and 59–61% across the three largest ethnicity strata (Caucasian 60.9%, African American 60.2%, Hispanic 59.3%; Supplementary Table S4). Three ethnicity strata in one US ICU dataset do not support a fairness claim; what can be said is narrower. The engine contains no race or ethnicity term: the registry text has no occurrence of 'race', 'ethnic', 'African', 'Black', 'Caucasian' or 'Hispanic', 'white' appears only in 'white blood cell', and the engine's optional `ethnicity` context field is parsed but never read by any rule or layer.

### Robustness

The release passed the non-regression gate (safety batteries = 353 adversarial vignettes with an expected diagnosis and maximum rank, 755 perturbation pairs that must keep the expected rank under 3% analyte noise and unit twins, and 1,000 synthetic guideline-normal panels that must yield no disease; defined in Supplementary Note 4: no vignette, perturbation pair or healthy panel that passed in the prior release may fail; both residual failures below pre-date v7): 689/689 unit tests, 352/353 adversarial vignettes (the one failure, alcoholic pancreatitis ranked second behind alcohol-use disorder, was deliberately not patched with a case-specific rule), 753/755 perturbation pairs (752/755 in release 14.1; all failures, then and now, are replicates of one vignette—thiazide-associated hyponatraemia under 3% noise—of which v7 recovered one replicate and two remain; the full failure list is in Supplementary Note 4) and the SI/conventional unit-twin battery.

## Discussion

A guideline-derived rule engine with no training data recovered 61% of clinician-listed ICU diagnoses in five suggestions from laboratory values alone (78% on the post-hoc panel-supported subset), and reproduced screening AUROCs across three NHANES cycles (0.88–0.99 for six targets; 0.87–0.88 for ALT elevation), weighted and unweighted, while asserting disease in at most one adult per healthy cohort. In a blinded paired comparison on identical inputs the frozen engine and an unnamed frontier LLM were statistically indistinguishable on the pre-specified metric (60.7% vs 59.6%), with the LLM stronger at rank 1 (28.2% vs 24.0%) and the engine at coverage (recall@10 72.0% vs 65.1%; labels absent from the list 21.8% vs 34.9%); the engine's advantages are determinism, local execution and traceability to a cited rule, not accuracy. It failed, as pre-specified, to predict sepsis.

Three findings shape how these numbers should be read. First, the apparent ~60% ceiling is largely a property of the reference standard: a quarter of admission-level labels were not inferable from the first panel, and a supervised model trained on the labels themselves did not exceed 0.82 AUROC for outcome-defined groups. The panel-supported metric, though pre-specified, was defined after the ceiling was observed and is structurally optimistic; we present it alongside, not instead of, the flat metric. Second, the untrained engine matched supervised models wherever a guideline definition exists and lost wherever the label is an outcome (sepsis, bleeding, withdrawal) that the model learns from hidden correlates. This is the expected division of labour between knowledge and learning, and it argues for hybrid rather than replacement designs. Third, the LLM was better at rank 1 (28.2% vs 24.0%) and worse at coverage (recall@10 65.1% vs 72.0%; it omitted 34.9% of labels against 21.8%), and its self-agreement on re-reading was only moderate (κ = 0.69, recall@5 varying by ±5 points—more than the engine–LLM difference). Per group (Fig. 6) the differences are small where a single analyte defines the label (renal impairment 83% LLM vs 81% engine; acute kidney injury 76% vs 80%; sepsis 45% vs 42%) and large where a multi-analyte pattern must be assembled or where the LLM prefers a broader term: the engine led for gastrointestinal bleeding (84% vs 50%), diabetes (50% vs 37%) and hypokalaemia (59% vs 41%), the LLM for metabolic acidosis (79% vs 50%), hyperkalaemia (90% vs 70%) and DKA (83% vs 73%), where the engine's covering-diagnosis suppression demotes the finding once a cause is named. The two systems make different errors, which is itself a reason to study them together rather than to declare a winner. Fourth, the ablation locates the result in the rule-engine vote and the ~900 curated syndrome constellations (written by the first author from guideline criteria and, like the registry, not independently validated); the 33,000-rule registry adds about one point with the syndrome layer present and three without it, so it is partly redundant with the syndrome layer rather than inert. We therefore describe Hemoglyph as a guideline-*transcribed* engine rather than a guideline-derived one: its knowledge was transcribed from guidelines by one non-clinician author with AI assistance, and the components that carry the result are exactly the ones that an independent audit has not yet examined; the result should be read as what such a transcription achieves, not as what the guidelines themselves would achieve: with the AI-drafted rules off, top-5 recall moved by under one point. This both limits the unaudited rules' influence on what we report and removes registry scale as a selling point; the Bayesian, fuzzy, knowledge-graph and risk-model layers were, on this cohort, close to inert. Part of that inertness is by construction: the expert floor (fused score ≥ 0.90 × rule vote) means the rule layer can be modulated but not vetoed, a deliberate bias towards explicit guideline criteria that trades the possibility of a learned layer correcting a wrong rule for the guarantee that every surfaced diagnosis is traceable to one; the sensitivity analysis (Table 5, uniform and rule-light weights within 1.4 points) shows the result does not depend on the exact weights, but it cannot show what a design without the floor would do. We note that our first submission reported superiority against an earlier engine version; re-scoring against the frozen release with clustered inference reduced that to parity, and we report the change. Fifth, a prevalence-ranking baseline is a sobering comparator: it reaches recall@5 of 51% and overtakes the engine at k = 10, because ten slots cover nearly every common ICU group. The engine's measurable contribution is therefore the ordering (NDCG@5 56.5% vs 39.0%; recall@3 47.9% vs 36.1%) and the per-stay specificity of what it asserts (precision@5 14.2% vs 10.8%), not list coverage, and precision against an incomplete problem list remains low in absolute terms.

Our evaluation design addresses several criticisms levelled at recent LLM benchmarks—unstable metrics, leakage and post-hoc selection [17,18]. Releases were frozen with manifests; quarantine data were run once; a round that failed is reported with its original criteria, and the subsequent change to non-inferiority criteria—made after seeing the failure—is disclosed as a weakening of the design; and the sepsis analysis that was added after a disappointing result is labelled post-hoc. The cost of correctness is visible: moving from the product release (13.4.2) to v7 cost three points of flat recall, almost entirely because a single creatinine no longer asserts CKD and a normal leukocyte count no longer yields a syndrome. We consider that trade accepted and disclose it.

Compared with Isabel and DXplain, which take symptoms and have been evaluated on 36–201 cases [13–16,30], this study isolates laboratory-only reasoning on more than 20,000 real encounters and releases its knowledge base under an open licence at a time when the main public executable-guideline repository has been withdrawn. Because our rules were drafted with AI assistance, the open release is a precondition for—not a substitute for—expert audit. We regard the knowledge base as a living artefact: every leaf carries a source locator, so a clinician can accept, amend or reject a rule and the change is versioned; the provenance audit already identifies the 34 sources that cannot be traced and the 28 rules that depend on them.

Where might such an engine be used? Not as an autonomous diagnostician. Its natural positions are the post-analytical step of a laboratory information system—interpretive comments and critical-pattern alerts generated locally, without transmitting patient data—and as a reproducible, auditable baseline against which LLM-based interpretation can be measured. The determinism and traceability that make it attractive for regulated settings are precisely the properties that the LLM in our comparison lacked (test–retest κ = 0.69), and the two architectures could be combined: a language model to extract and normalise free-text results (the step at which our automatic parser lost half the PMC cases) and a rule engine to reason over them.

Several design decisions deserve justification. We chose laboratory-only inputs as the primary arm because documented history inflates recall mainly by echoing the diagnosis already written down (+23 points for labels named in the history versus +1 point otherwise); evaluations that feed problem lists to the system under test risk measuring transcription, not inference. We scored recall against a clinician problem list rather than adjudicated diagnoses because adjudication at this scale was beyond our means; we quantified rather than hid the resulting ceiling. We did not tune the engine to the sepsis task after the pre-specified result because an outcome-fitted rule would have been a supervised model in disguise; the guideline-sourced SIRS/qSOFA rules that were added under Amendment 15 were developed on set A and validated once on set B2.

What would change the evidence level? First, a rule audit by independent clinicians on a random sample, with inter-rater agreement reported; second, the credentialed full eICU and MIMIC-IV releases, on which the released scripts run unchanged; third, a clinician-in-the-loop study measuring diagnostic accuracy, time and automation bias with and without the engine, for which a crossover protocol has been drafted. Until then, the claims of this paper are deliberately bounded to retrospective, laboratory-only performance.
A post-freeze analysis of twelve reviewer-suggested improvements (Supplementary Note 7) found that the two that promised the largest gains—truncating the differential to raise precision, and suppressing covering diagnoses—would have traded recall for precision one-for-one or asserted diagnoses without their guideline criteria; the remaining recall ceiling on eICU is set by panels that lack the defining analyte and by the problem-list reference standard rather than by the reasoning layer, which is why we moved the supervised sepsis component outside the engine instead of training the engine.

### Limitations

The knowledge base has not been audited rule-by-rule by clinicians; the components that carry the result—272 anchor rules and ~900 syndrome constellations—were written by a non-clinician first author and have not been independently audited; 34 sources lack a locator. The Bayesian priors and likelihood ratios are hand-coded from literature without a per-value citation table. No version-control history exists for the development period, so the claim that no change was outcome-fitted rests on the dated change log alone. Only the open demo subsets of eICU and MIMIC-IV were used; the reference standards are admission-level problem lists and ICD codes, not adjudicated panel-level diagnoses. The LLM comparison is an author-run self-baseline accessed through an agent platform that does not disclose the model or version, so the LLM arm is not reproducible against a named model; it used two prompts, no retrieval augmentation and no clinician adjudication of either output, and the model's training data may include the public eICU demo. The quarantine criteria were changed from superiority to non-inferiority after round 1 failed. The panel-supported metric is post hoc. The optical-character-recognition front end was not evaluated. Laboratory-defined NHANES labels overlap the inputs. Raw scores are uncalibrated. No clinician-in-the-loop evaluation was performed; a pre-registered crossover reader study with an automation-bias endpoint is in preparation. Iranian populations and non-English laboratory conventions were not evaluated. Engine v8 and the sepsis companion are post-freeze development work: they have not been through a quarantine round, the companion's boosting model was regularised once after a look at set B, and none of the frozen numbers in this paper derive from them. The first author is a secondary-school student with a financial interest in the commercial product, and the system, including 99.2% of the rules, was developed with AI-agent assistance, declared below. Only demo subsets of eICU and MIMIC-IV were used; the external cohorts are small and ILPD discrimination was modest (0.704). Hemoglyph is a research prototype and has no regulatory clearance.

### Conclusion

An open, offline, guideline-derived rule engine interpreted structured laboratory panels close to the ceiling the first panel allows, reproduced population-screening performance across independent survey cycles under internally pre-specified (not publicly registered) criteria that were weakened from superiority to non-inferiority after a failed first quarantine round, and was indistinguishable from an unnamed frontier LLM on a blinded paired primary metric, while remaining auditable. The components that carry the result are exactly those an independent clinical audit has not yet examined, and the LLM arm is not reproducible against a named model. Its limits—outcome-defined syndromes, uncalibrated scores and an unaudited AI-drafted rule base—are stated so that they can be measured and closed.

## Methods

### Engine

Hemoglyph (`analyse_gidlines.py` [sic—historical file name], v7) processes a panel in five stages: normalisation (UCUM units, aliases, age/sex reference intervals, 100 derived indices); a rule layer over registry 14.1.0 (33,183 executable leaves, where a leaf is one registry record with a machine-evaluable condition on at least one analyte, a disease identifier and an `executable: true` flag—retired, template and empty-expression records are kept for provenance but never evaluated—a count that moved from 34,005 in release 13.1.0 through 33,230 (13.3.0), 33,170 (13.4.2) and 33,178 (14.0.0) as 1,307 rules were retired and 485 guideline-sourced rules were added, Supplementary Table S10; each with analyte conditions, population gates, evidence role—diagnostic criterion weight 6, supportive or screening weight 3—disease identifier and source); an expert syndrome layer (~900 constellations with anchor strengths 0.95/0.88/0.80/0.72/0.45, operators absent/between/neg/present and context predicates for pregnancy, age, sex, medication, condition, family history and encounter); fixed-weight fusion (weights 0.35 rule engine, 0.25 Bayesian, 0.20 fuzzy, 0.10 knowledge graph, 0.10 risk models, set by the first author as a judgement that explicit criteria should dominate, with four context profiles, e.g. toxicology 0.55 for rules; no weight was derived from or tuned on any evaluation dataset, and Table 5 reports the sensitivity to them) with an expert floor (the fused score of a diagnosis cannot fall below 0.90 × the rule-layer vote, so that the four other layers can modulate but never veto a guideline rule). The Bayesian layer has 75 disease nodes with hand-coded prevalence priors (constants or age bands, e.g. type 2 diabetes 0.08, CKD 0.10) and hand-coded likelihood ratios for 68 of them, taken from diagnostic-accuracy literature cited in code comments (e.g. lipase ≥ 3 × ULN, LR+ 18 for acute pancreatitis); none is estimated from data, so the engine remains untrained in the sense that no parameter was fitted to any patient record; and ranking hygiene (causal attribution via a disease–finding explanation graph, covering-diagnosis suppression of explained findings, KDIGO chronicity, UDMI acuity, maximum 25 entries). Engine changes between releases (C1–C16) are listed with dates in Supplementary Table S1; none was fitted to outcome data and no rule was written for a specific missed case. This is supported by the dated change log and by the fact that every change is expressed as a guideline citation plus a synthetic vignette rather than a parameter value; no version-control history exists for the development period, so the statement cannot be audited by commit diff and is offered as a documented process claim, not a verified one.

### Knowledge base construction and provenance

'AI assistance' means the following. For each guideline source the first author prompted a general-purpose LLM with the source text or citation and a fixed JSON schema; the model returned candidate rule records (analyte, operator, threshold, unit, population gate, evidence role, disease identifier, source locator). These records were not individually edited by a human: they were accepted or rejected by automated audits (schema validity, analyte exists in the 2,045-analyte feature registry, unit convertible, threshold within physiological range, no duplicate or contradictory record, context gate present where the guideline requires one, source locator resolvable) and carry the status `candidate_requires_specialist_validation`. The 272 anchor rules were written or line-edited by hand by the first author from the primary guideline text and carry higher weight; the 13 demoted anchors and all rules retired between releases were removed by rule-level review of defect reports, not by outcome data. No record of the proportion of candidate rules that a human read exists, and we therefore describe the bulk as AI-drafted and automatically audited, not human-reviewed. Sources: KDIGO, ADA, WHO, ACC/AHA, EASL, ATA, ESC, CLSI, Tietz and others listed in the registry. Source locators were verified with `registry_source_verify.py` (Crossref resolution, HTTP reachability). Counts by release: 13.3.0, 33,230 rules/747 sources; 13.4.2, 33,170/781; 14.1.0, 33,183/785.

### Pre-specification, freezes and quarantine

The SAP (Supplementary Note 1) fixed metrics, label maps, thresholds and comparators; 15 amendments are dated and justified. Freezes v14 and v15 recorded SHA-256 manifests. Quarantine round 1 (MIMIC-IV demo; NHANES 2013–14; sepsis set B) used superiority criteria and failed; round 2 (NHANES 2015–16; sepsis set B2; healthy sweep with a new seed) used non-inferiority criteria specified before execution and passed. Two data-loader defects discovered during round 2 (string patient identifiers; a dropped derived NHANES variable) were fixed before scoring without touching the engine and are documented.

### Datasets and reference standards

eICU-CRD demo v2.0.1 [19]: 2,520 stays → 2,512 adults → 2,314 with ≥ 8 analytes in the first panel; reference = clinician problem list mapped by regular expressions to 31 groups (audit on 400 stays: precision 100%, sensitivity 98.3%; per-group values in Supplementary Table S9). MIMIC-IV demo [20]: 235 admissions, ICD-10 reference. NHANES 2013–14, 2015–16, 2017–18: 3,000 randomly sampled adults per cycle with biochemistry and CBC; references per ADA, KDIGO, WHO and ACG criteria and self-reported diagnoses. PhysioNet/CinC 2019 [24]: set A 4,871 patients (439 sepsis), set B 4,558 (268), set B2 4,991; Sepsis-3 onset label; the post-hoc concurrent analysis (labs and vitals within ±6 h of onset) used 247 cases and 600 dwell-time-matched controls drawn from set B2 only, which is why Table 3 reports it for B2 alone. UCI thyroid0387 [22], UCI HCV [21], ILPD [23], PMC-Patients 50 case reports [17]. Participant flow in Supplementary Table S3.

### Metrics

Primary: flat recall@k of clinician-listed diagnoses (k = 3, 5, 10). Secondary: two-tier recall; panel-supported recall (labels whose first panel met a guideline criterion fixed in `panel_supported_metric.py` before scoring; groups without a single criterion excluded); per-group AUROC of the group score. Screening: AUROC and false-assertion rate in all-normal adults. Sepsis: AUROC of the sepsis-group score. Calibration: calibration slope and intercept, ECE (10 equal-frequency bins), Brier with cluster-bootstrap CI, log-loss and decision curves before and after cross-fitted isotonic regression [29]. Ablation: the frozen engine with one component disabled or altered per arm (each of five fusion layers, syndrome layer, registry, anchor-only registry, `unverified_source` rules removed, fusion-weight and shrinkage settings), scored on the same 2,314 stays.

### Comparators

B1, raw key-analyte threshold; B2, gradient boosting and logistic regression trained on the same analytes, five-fold out-of-fold; registry-off ablation; LLM: an unnamed frontier model run by the authors through a chat interface on 2026-10-21 (n = 60), 2026-10-22 (n = 100) and 2026-10-23 (n = 240), re-read 2026-10-24 and alternative prompt 2026-10-25; the interface exposes no temperature or sampling parameters, so these are the provider defaults; the LLM was accessed through the Arena.ai agent platform, which routes requests among several frontier model families (including GPT, Claude and Gemini) and does not expose the specific model or version to the user; 'unnamed frontier LLM' therefore denotes a capability tier, not a verified identity, and the comparison cannot be reproduced against a named model version—a limitation stated in the Discussion; blinded to labels, with a fixed prompt requesting ten ranked diagnoses from the identical panel, age and sex; 40 panels re-read for test–retest and read with an alternative prompt; scoring against the frozen engine v7 by the same label map and script. Prompts, outputs and the full protocol are released (Supplementary Note 2).

### Post-freeze development version (v8)

Engine v8 (2026-10-26) differs from the frozen v7 in three presentation- or index-level additions—MELD/MELD-Na (Kamath 2001; Kim 2008; OPTN formula with sodium clamped to 125–137 mmol/L and creatinine capped at 4 mg/dL) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio as derived patterns with `supports = []`, and a `confidence_band`/`concise` field on each differential entry (bands ≥ 0.70 high, 0.60–0.69 moderate, 0.50–0.59 low, otherwise consider; descriptive, calibrated on the development set only)—and in registry 14.2.0, which re-sources 28 rules (9 source records) with Crossref-resolved DOIs and changes no rule logic, threshold, weight or executable flag (`eval/registry_patches/patch_14_2_0.py`). The sepsis companion (`eval/sepsis_ml/sepsis_risk_module.py`) uses the 5,000-patient public subsets of PhysioNet/CinC 2019; features are listed in the script; the logistic model is class-balanced L2 (C = 0.05); the gradient-boosting reference uses depth ≤ 4, ≤ 15 leaves, ≥ 300 samples per leaf, L2 = 5 and early stopping on 15% of set A. The alert threshold for the utility score was chosen on set A over a grid of score quantiles. Disclosure: the boosting hyper-parameters were tightened once after a first evaluation on set B showed over-fitting (training AUROC 0.997, set B 0.719); set B2 was not opened until the final configuration. Patient-level AUROC uses the maximum score over hours before the shifted label for septic patients and over all hours for non-septic patients; horizon AUROC compares the score exactly h hours before onset with a random hour of each non-septic patient. The v8 gate (`eval/results/gate_v8/`) repeats the v7 gate (three vignette batteries, 1,000-panel healthy sweep, 755-pair perturbation battery, unit tests, eICU labs-only run) with a pre-stated non-inferiority margin of 0.5 recall points.

### Statistics and software

Labels are nested in stays (ICC 0.04); eICU proportions and paired differences use stay-cluster bootstrap (4,000 resamples) and the cluster-adjusted McNemar test [31,32]; Wilson intervals elsewhere; 1,000-sample bootstrap or DeLong for AUROC; NHANES AUROCs additionally weighted by examination weights; Benjamini–Hochberg across per-group tests. Python 3.11/3.13, numpy, pandas, scikit-learn. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI [25], adapted for a system that is not fitted to data (model-training items answered "not applicable" with justification), with the DECIDE-AI early-stage items consulted for the Limitations; the completed checklist is Supplementary Note 5.7.
